How to calculate fertilizer for your lawn

Lawn fertilizer calculations follow a simple two-step formula used by university extension services across the world:

Step 1: Calculate product needed per 1,000 sq ft

Product per 1,000 sq ft = Desired N rate ÷ (N% ÷ 100)

For example, with a 10-10-10 fertilizer and a target of 1 lb nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft:

1 ÷ 0.10 = 10 lbs of product per 1,000 sq ft

Step 2: Calculate total product for your lawn

Total product = Product per 1,000 sq ft × (Lawn area ÷ 1,000)

For a 5,000 sq ft lawn: 10 × 5 = 50 lbs of 10-10-10 fertilizer.

This calculator does both steps automatically and also shows you how much actual nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium your lawn will receive.

Understanding the N-P-K ratio

Every fertilizer bag shows three numbers, such as 10-10-10 or 32-0-4. These represent the percentage by weight of three key nutrients:

  • Nitrogen (N) — drives green colour and leaf growth. This is the primary nutrient for lawn fertilisation. Nitrogen deficiency causes yellowing and slow growth; excess nitrogen causes excessive top growth, thatch buildup and increased disease susceptibility.
  • Phosphorus (P₂O₅) — supports root development, flowering and seeding. Most established lawns need little to no phosphorus, which is why many lawn fertilizers are "0" in the middle number. Starter fertilizers for new lawns or overseeding typically contain higher phosphorus.
  • Potassium (K₂O) — builds stress tolerance, drought resistance and winter hardiness. Winterizer fertilizers for fall application are usually higher in potassium.

The ratio tells you the relative proportions. A 3-1-2 ratio (like 15-5-10) is ideal for maintenance fertilisation. A 1-0-1 ratio is common for summer stress. The higher the first number, the less product you need to deliver the same amount of nitrogen.

How much fertilizer per 1,000 square feet?

The standard recommendation across university extension services is 1 pound of actual nitrogen per 1,000 square feet per application for quick-release fertilizers. For slow-release or controlled-release products, the rate can be higher — often 1.5 pounds per 1,000 sq ft — because the nitrogen releases slowly and is less likely to burn the grass.

How much lime or sulfur to adjust soil pH

Soil pH affects nutrient availability. Most lawn grasses prefer pH 6.0 to 7.0. When pH drops below 6.0 (acidic), nutrients like phosphorus and calcium become less available. When it rises above 7.5 (alkaline), iron and manganese become less available, causing yellowing.

Lime raises pH. Application rates per 1,000 sq ft to raise pH by 1.0 unit:

  • Sandy soil: 50 lbs
  • Loam soil: 80 lbs
  • Clay soil: 120 lbs

Rates over 50 lbs per 1,000 sq ft should be split into two applications 4 to 6 months apart. Pelletized lime is grass-safe and can be watered in immediately.

Elemental sulfur lowers pH. Apply no more than 10 lbs per 1,000 sq ft in a single pass, and re-test each season. Sulfur works more slowly than lime because soil bacteria must convert it first.

How much topdressing to apply

Topdressing is a thin layer of compost, sand or topsoil spread over the lawn to level low spots, improve soil structure and encourage thatch breakdown. The recommended depth is 0.25 to 0.5 inches per application.

The formula for volume:

Cubic yards = (Area in sq ft × Depth in inches) ÷ 324

The constant 324 converts square feet to cubic yards (27 cubic feet per cubic yard × 12 inches per foot). For example, 5,000 sq ft at 0.25 inches deep requires (5,000 × 0.25) ÷ 324 = 3.86 cubic yards.

One cubic yard covers about 324 square feet at 1 inch deep, or 648 square feet at 0.5 inches deep.

How to calibrate your fertilizer spreader

Spreader calibration is the difference between a professional result and a striped, patchy lawn. Even if you calculate the right amount of fertilizer, an incorrectly calibrated spreader will apply too much in some areas and too little in others.

  1. Mark out a test area of a known size — 400 sq ft is convenient (20 ft × 20 ft).
  2. Fill the spreader with a known weight of fertilizer. Weigh it before you start.
  3. Spread over the test area at your intended setting.
  4. Weigh the spreader again to see how much product was used.
  5. Calculate your actual application rate: (Product used ÷ Test area) × 1,000 = pounds per 1,000 sq ft.
  6. If the rate is too high, lower the spreader setting and repeat. If too low, raise it.
  7. Repeat until your actual rate matches your target rate within 10%.

Common lawn fertilizer mistakes

  • Guessing the application rate. Too much fertilizer burns the grass; too little wastes money and underfeeds the lawn. Always calculate.
  • Ignoring grass type. Bermuda needs twice the nitrogen of Centipede. Using the same rate for every lawn leads to problems.
  • Applying at the wrong time. Cool-season grasses should be fertilised in spring and fall, warm-season grasses in summer. Fertilising at the wrong time encourages weeds and disease.
  • Not calibrating the spreader. A spreader set to 5 on one machine may apply a different rate than a spreader set to 5 on another. Calibration is essential.
  • Forgetting the spreader buffer. Overlap and waste mean you need 5 to 10% more product than the theoretical calculation suggests.
  • Applying too much nitrogen at once. Never exceed 1 lb of quick-release nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft in a single application. Split higher annual targets across multiple applications.
  • Ignoring soil pH. If pH is out of range, fertilizer is wasted because nutrients are locked up and unavailable to the grass.

First calculate total pounds of product needed using the formula above, then divide by the weight of the bag you are buying. For example, if you need 50 pounds and the fertilizer comes in 40-pound bags, you would buy 2 bags. Always round up to ensure you do not run out mid-application.

Cool-season grasses (Kentucky Bluegrass, Fescue, Ryegrass) grow most actively in spring and fall. They typically need 3 to 4 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft per year, split across 3 to 4 applications. Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, Zoysia, St. Augustine) grow most actively in summer and need 2 to 3 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft per year.

Overseeding an existing lawn typically requires 2 to 5 pounds of grass seed per 1,000 square feet, roughly half the rate of seeding a brand-new lawn. Tall fescue and perennial ryegrass need about 3 to 4 pounds per 1,000 sq ft for overseeding, Kentucky bluegrass needs 1 to 1.5 pounds, and warm-season grasses like Bermuda need under 1 pound.
