How GPA is calculated

GPA is a credit-weighted average, not a plain average. The formula used by virtually every US college and university is:

GPA = Σ (grade points × credit hours) ÷ Σ credit hours

Grade points come from the grading scale. On the standard 4.0 scale, an A is 4.0, a B is 3.0, a C is 2.0, a D is 1.0 and an F is 0.0. Plus and minus grades carry intermediate values: an A− is 3.7, a B+ is 3.3, a C− is 1.7 and so on.

The "credit-weighted" part is what makes GPA different from a plain average. A 4-credit calculus course counts four times as much as a 1-credit lab. If you get an A in the lab and a C in the calculus course, your GPA will be dominated by the calculus grade — as it should be, since the calculus course represents four times the work.

Weighted vs unweighted GPA

The two most common GPA types in US high schools are unweighted and weighted.

Unweighted GPA

Treats every course equally on the 4.0 scale. An A in a regular class and an A in an AP class are both 4.0. Unweighted GPA is the most widely reported and compared number. It never exceeds 4.0 (or the top of whatever scale your school uses).

Weighted GPA

Adds bonus points for advanced courses to reward students who take on more challenging work. The most common bonus scheme is:

Course levelBonus (4.0 → 5.0 scale)Bonus (5.0 → 6.0 scale)
Regular+0.0+0.0
Honors+0.5+1.0
AP / IB / Dual Enrollment+1.0+2.0

With this scheme, an A in an AP class on a weighted 5.0 scale is worth 5.0 instead of 4.0. Students who take many AP courses can graduate with a weighted GPA above 4.5 or even 5.0.

Most selective colleges consider both weighted and unweighted GPA and often recalculate using their own formula. This tool shows both side by side.

Cumulative GPA vs semester GPA

Semester GPA covers a single term. Cumulative GPA covers every term combined. Cumulative GPA is calculated by summing the quality points from every course you have taken — across all semesters — and dividing by the total graded credits.

Cumulative GPA is the number that appears on your transcript and the one colleges, scholarships and employers ask for. A strong semester can pull up a weaker cumulative GPA, but only in proportion to the credits earned. This is why raising a cumulative GPA gets harder over time: as you accumulate more credits, each new semester carries less weight in the average.

Reverse GPA calculation: what grades do I need?

One of the most useful features of this tool is the target GPA calculation. If you know your current cumulative GPA and how many credits you have left, you can compute the average grade you need on remaining courses to reach a target.

The formula is:

Required average = (Target GPA × Total final credits − Current quality points) ÷ Remaining credits

If the required average exceeds your maximum GPA (for example, 4.3 when the scale caps at 4.0), the target is mathematically impossible without additional credits. This tool shows that honestly rather than hiding it.

How retaking a course affects your GPA

Retake policies vary widely between schools, and the difference matters:

  • Grade replacement: The new grade replaces the old one. Most common at US universities — often with a limit on how many times you can repeat.
  • Average of both attempts: Both grades count, so the retake has half the effect of a first attempt.
  • Higher grade counted: The better of the two is used, and the lower is ignored. Most generous policy.

This tool's Retake Mode tab lets you model all three policies against your actual current GPA and credits, so you can see exactly how much a retake would help.

Multi-scale support: 4.0, 5.0, 10.0 and percentage

Different countries and institutions use different GPA scales. This tool supports the four most common:

4.0 scale (US, Canada)

The standard US scale. A = 4.0, B = 3.0, C = 2.0, D = 1.0, F = 0.0. Plus/minus grades carry intermediate values. Weighted variants extend the scale to 5.0 or 6.0.

5.0 scale (weighted US, some Canadian provinces)

Extends the 4.0 scale to accommodate AP, IB and Honors bonus points. Regular courses still cap at 4.0; advanced courses can reach 5.0.

10.0 scale (India CGPA)

Widely used in Indian universities and CBSE. A CGPA of 10.0 is equivalent to roughly 95–100 percent; 9.0 is about 85–95 percent; 8.0 is about 75–85 percent. Conversion formulas vary by institution.

Percentage scale

Used in the UK, Europe, and many other countries. Percentage to 4.0 GPA conversion is approximate: roughly 90–100 percent = 4.0, 80–89 percent = 3.0–3.9, 70–79 percent = 2.0–2.9, below 70 percent = below 2.0.

Why GPA matters

GPA is the single most important quantitative measure in academic applications. Research from the National Association for College Admission Counseling consistently ranks GPA as the top factor in admissions decisions, ahead of test scores, essays and extracurriculars.

GPA also affects:

  • Scholarships and financial aid. Most merit-based awards have minimum GPA requirements.
  • Graduate school admissions. A 3.0 is the common minimum for most master's programmes; competitive programmes often require 3.5 or higher.
  • Internships and first jobs. Many employers screen candidates by GPA cutoff, especially for competitive entry-level roles.
  • Study abroad and exchange programmes. GPA thresholds are standard for eligibility.
  • Athletic eligibility. NCAA and NAIA both require minimum GPAs for competition.
  • Honours and Dean's List. Most universities publish GPA thresholds for these recognitions.

Common GPA mistakes to avoid

  • Averaging semester GPAs. You cannot simply average semester GPAs to get cumulative GPA — you must weight each semester by its credit hours.
  • Using unweighted GPA to report to colleges that recalculate. Check each college's policy; many use a specific formula that may exclude or adjust certain courses.
  • Forgetting Pass/Fail courses. P/F courses earn credits but do not affect GPA. This tool excludes them automatically.
  • Ignoring +/- modifiers. An A− (3.7) and an A (4.0) are different. Precision matters when you are close to a cutoff.
  • Not checking retake policy. Retake rules vary so widely that two students with the same grades can end up with very different GPAs after a retake. Always verify your school's policy.
  • Calculating mid-semester with incomplete grades. In-progress courses are not yet factored into GPA. Only completed, graded courses count.

How is GPA calculated?

GPA is a credit-weighted average: each course grade is converted to grade points on a scale (usually 4.0), multiplied by the course credit hours to give quality points, and the total quality points are divided by the total graded credits. A 4-credit course therefore affects GPA four times as much as a 1-credit course.

What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?

Unweighted GPA treats every course equally on the 4.0 scale: an A is 4.0 whether it is a regular class or an AP class. Weighted GPA adds bonus points for advanced courses, typically +0.5 for Honors and +1.0 for AP or IB, so an A in an AP class may be worth 5.0 instead of 4.0.

What is cumulative GPA?

Cumulative GPA is the GPA across all your semesters or terms combined. It is calculated by summing the quality points from every course you have taken and dividing by the total graded credits.
