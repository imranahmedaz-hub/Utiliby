What this free compound interest calculator does

Albert Einstein is often credited with calling compound interest the eighth wonder of the world, although there is no evidence he said it. What is certain is that interest earning interest is the engine behind most long-term savings, retirement accounts and investment growth. A compound interest calculator lets you see that effect in numbers instead of guessing.

This free online compound interest calculator shows the future value of a starting amount with optional monthly or yearly deposits, any compounding frequency from annual to continuous, and a yearly deposit increase to model pay rises. It also shows the value in today's money after inflation, a stacked chart of deposits versus interest, a year-by-year table you can download, a goal planner that tells you how long you need or how much to save, a frequency comparison and a Rule of 72 tool. Everything runs in your browser.

How to use the compound interest calculator

  1. Enter the starting amount, rate and time.
  2. Choose the compounding frequency. Monthly is a common default for savings accounts.
  3. Add regular deposits. Pick monthly or yearly, and start or end of period.
  4. Optionally add a yearly deposit increase and inflation.
  5. Read the chart. Blue shows what you put in and green shows interest.
  6. Use the other tabs to plan a goal, compare frequencies or apply the Rule of 72.

The compound interest formula

The classic formula is A = P (1 + r/n)^(nt). P is the principal, r the annual rate as a decimal, n the number of times interest compounds each year and t the number of years. For $10,000 at 7 percent compounded monthly for 10 years, A = 10,000 x (1 + 0.07/12)^120 = $20,096.61. With annual compounding the answer is $19,671.51, and with continuous compounding, A = P e^(rt), it is $20,137.53.

Regular deposits are handled by stepping the balance forward month by month at the equivalent monthly rate, adding each deposit as it is made. With $500 deposited at the end of every month, the same 7 percent and 10 years gives $106,639.02, of which $70,000 is the start plus deposits and $36,639 is interest.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Slightly. The more often interest compounds, the faster the balance grows, because interest is added to the balance sooner. Yet the effect is modest. $10,000 at 7 percent for 10 years ends at $19,672 compounded annually, $20,097 monthly, $20,136 daily and $20,138 continuously. Moving from annual to monthly adds about $425, while moving from monthly to continuous adds only $41. A one percentage point higher rate or five more years does far more than any compounding choice.

The power of regular deposits and time

Two levers drive compound growth: how much you put in and how long it has to work. With 30 years at 7 percent, $500 a month grows to about $691,000 from just $190,000 of deposits and the $10,000 start, so interest provides most of the final balance. Starting ten years earlier usually beats saving much more later. The chart makes that visible, with the green interest area overtaking the blue deposit area in later years.

Real returns and inflation

A balance of $106,639 in ten years will buy less than $106,639 buys today. If inflation averages 3 percent a year, the purchasing power equals about $79,349 in today's money. Enter an inflation estimate to see both figures. Looking at the real value is a more honest way to judge whether a savings plan meets a goal.

The Rule of 72 and doubling time

The Rule of 72 estimates how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual return. At 7 percent that is 72 / 7 = 10.3 years, close to the exact 10.24 years. It works the other way too: to double in 8 years you need about 9 percent. The fourth tab gives the estimate, the exact figure and a table of common rates.

Using the goal planner

Enter a target such as 1,000,000 and the planner steps through your inputs to find how long it takes. With $10,000 to start, $500 a month and 7 percent, the balance passes $1,000,000 after about 34 years and 10 months. It also solves for the deposit needed to reach the target within your chosen time frame, which is useful for retirement or savings goals.

Frequently asked questions

What is a compound interest calculator?

A tool that shows how money grows when interest is added to the balance and then earns interest itself. Enter a starting amount, rate, time and optional deposits to see the future value.

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P (1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is the starting amount, r the annual rate, n the compounding periods per year and t the years. With regular deposits the future value of the deposits is added. For continuous compounding the formula is A = P e^(rt).

How much will $10,000 grow at 7% in 10 years?

Compounded monthly it becomes about $20,097. Compounded annually it becomes about $19,672. Adding $500 a month for the ten years takes it to about $106,639.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is paid on the original amount plus interest already earned, so growth accelerates over time.

Does compounding frequency matter?

A little. More frequent compounding gives slightly more, but the gap between daily and monthly is small. The rate, the time and the deposits matter far more. The frequency tab shows the difference for your numbers.

What is continuous compounding?

The mathematical limit as compounding becomes infinitely frequent, given by P e^(rt). At 7 percent for 10 years, $10,000 grows to $20,138 continuously versus $20,097 monthly.

How do regular deposits change the result?

Each deposit starts earning interest as soon as it is made, so steady saving usually adds far more than the starting amount over long periods. You can set monthly or yearly deposits and choose start or end of period.

Can the deposit increase every year?

Yes. Set a yearly deposit increase, for example 3 percent, to model pay rises. Each year's deposits are that much larger than the last.

How do I see the effect of inflation?

Enter an expected inflation rate. The tool shows the final balance in today's money by dividing by (1 + inflation) raised to the number of years.

How long will it take to reach my goal?

Use the goal planner. It steps through your inputs month by month until the balance reaches the target, and also solves for the deposit needed to get there within your chosen time.

What is the Rule of 72?

A shortcut: divide 72 by the annual return to estimate years to double. At 7 percent it is about 10.3 years. The exact figure is 10.24 years.

Is the Rule of 72 accurate?

It is close for rates between about 6 and 10 percent. At very low or very high rates the exact formula, ln 2 divided by ln(1 + r), is better. The tool shows both.

Is compound interest the same as APY?

APY (annual percentage yield) is the effective yearly rate after compounding. A 7 percent rate compounded monthly has an APY of about 7.23 percent.

Can I use it for debt?

The same maths applies to debt that compounds, such as credit cards. For fixed loans with payments, use a loan calculator, which handles repayment schedules.

Does it include taxes and fees?

No. It shows the pre-tax, pre-fee growth at a constant rate. Fees and taxes reduce real returns, so model a lower rate to be cautious.

Is my data stored or sent anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is stored or uploaded.

Is this compound interest calculator free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up.
