Instant, active dry and fresh yeast: what is the difference?

All three are the same living organism, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, sold in different forms. The differences are moisture, particle size and how they are used.

TypeAlso calledFormHow to useStrength
Instant yeastRapid-rise, quick-rise, bread machine, SAF RedFine dry granulesMix straight into the flourStrongest dry form (1 part)
Active dry yeastDry yeast, baker's yeastLarger dry granules with a coatingDissolve in warm water for 5 to 10 minutes firstAbout 1.25 parts
Fresh yeastCake yeast, compressed yeastMoist block, about 70% waterCrumble into liquid or flourAbout 3 parts by weight

Instant yeast has smaller granules and a higher share of live cells, which is why you need less of it. Fresh yeast contains water, so the same rising power takes more weight. Fresh yeast also has a short shelf life and is harder to find in many places, which is why recipes written for fresh yeast are often converted to dry.

Yeast conversion ratios and formulas

3:4 rule1 : 1.33 : 3Some manufacturers say to use three quarters as much instant as active dry.
Gentler fresh1 : 1.25 : 2.5Uses less fresh yeast; common in British and European recipes.

Formulas. Active dry = instant × 1.25. Fresh = instant × 3. Instant = active dry ÷ 1.25 = fresh ÷ 3. For example, a recipe that calls for 21 g of fresh yeast needs 7 g of instant yeast or about 8.75 g of active dry yeast.

Packets, teaspoons, tablespoons and cakes

Weights are the most accurate way to measure yeast, but most recipes use spoons and packets. These are the usual figures:

1 tablespoon dry yeast9.3 g
1 cake of fresh yeast (0.6 oz)17 g

Spoon measures vary with how packed the yeast is, so a level spoon is a guide, not an exact amount. For small batches, or for slow doughs where a few tenths of a gram matter, a kitchen scale that reads to 0.1 g is a worthwhile tool.

How much yeast per cup or gram of flour

Bakers describe yeast as a percentage of the flour weight. It makes recipes easy to scale and compare. The right amount depends on how long you want the dough to rise.

These are starting points. A cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 125 g, so 500 g is four cups. One packet of yeast (7 g) is plenty for up to about 4 cups of flour in a same-day dough, which is why most recipes call for a single packet.

How yeast amount and temperature change rise time

Yeast feeds on sugars in the flour and releases carbon dioxide and alcohol, which inflate and flavour the dough. The speed roughly doubles for every 15°F (8°C) of warmth between a cold refrigerator and a warm kitchen, and it is roughly proportional to the amount of yeast. Halve the yeast and the rise takes about twice as long; raise the temperature by 15°F and it takes about half as long.

The rise time tab uses this to estimate a total rise (bulk fermentation plus proof) of about 2 hours ÷ yeast percentage at 68°F (20°C), adjusted for temperature. At 1% instant yeast and 75°F that is about 1.5 hours; at 0.2% and 68°F it is about 10 hours; at 0.12% in the refrigerator it stretches to days. Treat the answer as a range, not a timer, and watch the dough: it should look puffy, jiggle slightly and spring back slowly when poked.

Proofing yeast and mixing it in

Active dry yeast is the form that benefits most from proofing: stir it into water at about 100 to 110°F (38 to 43°C) with a pinch of sugar and wait 5 to 10 minutes. A thick foam means the yeast is alive. Instant yeast does not need this; mix it into the flour. Fresh yeast can be crumbled into warm liquid or rubbed into the flour.

Liquid above about 120°F (49°C) starts to kill yeast, and salt in direct contact with it slows it, so mix the salt into the flour rather than dropping it onto the yeast. For long, cold ferments the water can be cool; for same-day doughs, aim for a dough temperature of about 75 to 80°F (24 to 27°C).

Yeast substitutes and what does not work

  • Baking powder or baking soda cannot replace yeast in a yeast dough. They are chemical leaveners for quick breads and cakes.
  • Sourdough starter can replace commercial yeast, but it is a different recipe with a much longer rise. A common rough guide is 20 to 30% of the flour weight as an active starter.
  • Brewer's yeast and nutritional yeast are not the same product. They are inactive or deactivated and will not make bread rise.
  • Expired yeast can be tested: stir a teaspoon into ½ cup of warm water with a pinch of sugar. No foam after 10 minutes means it is dead.

Storing yeast so it stays active

Heat, moisture and air shorten the life of yeast. Keep unopened packets in a cool, dry place. Once a jar or bag is open, close it tightly and keep it in the refrigerator (about 4 to 6 months) or the freezer (a year or more); frozen dry yeast can go straight from the freezer into the flour. Fresh yeast should be wrapped, kept cold and used within a week or two, and it can be frozen in small pieces if you accept some loss of strength.

Common yeast mistakes

  • Swapping fresh for dry by weight 1 for 1.
  • Using water that is too hot, which kills the yeast.
  • Dumping salt directly on the yeast.
  • Using too much yeast in a slow, cold ferment and ending up with an over-proofed dough.
  • Measuring yeast by heaped spoons rather than level ones.
  • Judging by the clock rather than the dough.

Troubleshooting a dough that is slow or too fast

If the dough is slow, check the temperature first, then the age of the yeast and the water temperature. Move the bowl somewhere about 78°F (26°C), such as an oven with only the light on. If the dough rose too fast and smells strongly of alcohol, it was warm or had too much yeast; shorten the next rise or reduce the yeast by a third. A dough that has collapsed has over-proofed; knead it briefly, shape it and give it a shorter second rise.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert active dry yeast to instant yeast?

Use about 25% less instant yeast than active dry. For example, 1 packet (2¼ teaspoons, 7 g) of active dry yeast is about 5 to 6 g, or roughly 1¾ teaspoons, of instant yeast. In practice many bakers simply swap them 1 for 1 and accept a slightly faster rise.

How do I convert instant yeast to active dry yeast?

Use about 25% more active dry yeast than instant (multiply by 1.25). One teaspoon of instant yeast is roughly 1¼ teaspoons of active dry. Active dry yeast is also best dissolved in warm water for 5 to 10 minutes first.

How much fresh yeast equals one packet of dry yeast?

One 0.6 oz (17 g) cake of fresh yeast replaces one 2¼ teaspoon (7 g) packet of active dry yeast in many recipes. By pure weight, fresh yeast is about 2.5 to 3 times the weight of instant yeast, so check which ratio your recipe uses.

How many teaspoons are in a packet of yeast?

A standard packet of dry yeast is about 7 grams, which is roughly 2¼ teaspoons. A 4 oz jar holds about 36 teaspoons, and a 1 lb bag about 144 teaspoons of dry yeast.

Is rapid-rise yeast the same as instant yeast?

They are very close. Rapid-rise, bread machine and instant yeast are all fine-grained strains that can be mixed straight into the flour without dissolving first. Some brands use slightly different blends, but you can treat them as the same in this calculator.

Do I need to proof (bloom) yeast?

Active dry yeast should be dissolved in warm water (about 100 to 110°F / 38 to 43°C) with a pinch of sugar for 5 to 10 minutes until foamy. Instant yeast can go straight into the dry ingredients. Fresh yeast is crumbled into liquid or flour. Proofing also proves the yeast is alive.

How much yeast should I use per cup of flour?

As a rule of thumb, about ¼ to ½ teaspoon of instant yeast per cup of flour (125 g) for a normal same-day dough, which is roughly 0.8 to 1.2% of the flour weight. Slow, cold or overnight doughs need far less, often 0.1 to 0.3%.

What is the yeast percentage in baker's math?

It is the weight of yeast divided by the weight of flour, times 100. A dough with 500 g of flour and 5 g of instant yeast has 1% yeast. The tool lets you pick a dough type, set your own percentage and see instant, active dry and fresh equivalents.

Can I use less yeast and let the dough rise longer?

Yes. Halving the yeast roughly doubles the fermentation time at the same temperature, and the flavour usually improves. Use the rise time tab to see how yeast percentage and dough temperature interact.

Can I use baking powder instead of yeast?

No. Baking powder and baking soda work by a chemical reaction and make quick breads rise, but they do not ferment dough, so the texture and flavour of a yeast bread will not be the same. Use yeast, or follow a recipe designed for chemical leavening.

Can I substitute sourdough starter for yeast?

You can convert a recipe, but it is a recipe change, not a simple swap: the starter adds flour and water and the rise takes much longer. A common rough starting point is replacing the yeast with 20 to 30% of the flour weight as active starter and extending the rise by many hours.

Why did my dough not rise?

The usual causes are old or dead yeast, liquid that was too hot (above about 120°F / 49°C kills yeast) or too cold, too much salt touching the yeast directly, and a cold kitchen. Test the yeast in warm sugar water, and check the dough temperature.

How long does yeast last, and how should I store it?

Unopened dry yeast lasts until the date on the packet. After opening, keep it in an airtight container in the refrigerator for about 4 to 6 months or in the freezer for a year or more. Fresh yeast lasts only 1 to 2 weeks in the refrigerator.

Does the conversion change for sweet doughs?

The ratios stay the same, but sweet and enriched doughs (lots of sugar, butter and eggs) rise more slowly, so they usually start with more yeast, about 1 to 1.5% of flour. Osmotolerant instant yeast is made for this and can be swapped in the same way as instant.

Is this calculator accurate for every brand?

It is a very good guide, but not an exact one. Brands differ in activity and how finely they are ground. Use the numbers as a start, then adjust the next bake by how fast the dough rose.
