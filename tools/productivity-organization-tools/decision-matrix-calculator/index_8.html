What this free decision matrix calculator does

Big decisions are hard because they involve many factors that pull in different directions. A job pays more but has a longer commute, a laptop is fast but heavy, a supplier is cheap but slow. When you weigh these in your head, the most vivid factor tends to win. A decision matrix, also known as a weighted scoring model or Pugh matrix, brings order to the problem: you list the options, list the criteria that matter, say how much each criterion matters and score every option against each one. The arithmetic then shows which option best matches your priorities.

This free online decision matrix calculator does the arithmetic and adds extras: a ranked table, a stacked bar chart that shows what each criterion contributes to each option, a flip for criteria where lower is better, and a robustness check that reveals if one criterion alone decides the winner. Everything updates as you type, and your work is saved in your browser.

How to make a decision with a weighted matrix

  1. State the decision. Write the question clearly, such as which laptop should I buy?
  2. List your options. Two to five serious ones are enough. Add or remove columns as needed.
  3. List the criteria. Note what matters: cost, quality, risk, speed, fit. Aim for five to eight, and avoid overlap.
  4. Weight the criteria. Give each a weight from 1 to 10 for its importance.
  5. Score each option. Rate each option against each criterion. Use evidence, not feelings, where possible.
  6. Read the result. Check the ranking, the chart and the robustness note.
  7. Sense-check. If the answer feels wrong, ask what the matrix is missing.

The formula behind the weighted score

The calculation is simple. For each option, the weighted score is the sum, across all criteria, of the weight times the score. If a criterion has weight 8 and an option scores 7, it contributes 56 points. The maximum possible score is the total of all weights multiplied by the top score on the scale, which is how the percentage of maximum is found. For criteria where lower is better, such as price, risk or effort, the score is flipped by subtracting it from the top of the scale plus one, so that a low raw figure becomes a high score. The ranking is the order of the totals.

How to set good weights and scores

Weights express priorities, so set them before you score the options, to avoid bending the weights to favour the option you secretly prefer. Rank the criteria from most to least important and then assign weights in that order. Give a single criterion a weight of 10 only if it is truly the most important. For scores, use a consistent rule for each criterion, such as the cheapest option gets 10 and others are scaled, or use measurable data like price, hours or ratings. If you are unsure, test the sensitivity by changing a weight by two points and see whether the winner changes. If it does, the decision is close and other factors may decide.

Strengths and limits of the method

A decision matrix makes assumptions visible, reduces the pull of one dramatic factor, helps groups discuss criteria instead of personalities, and leaves a record of why a choice was made. It also has limits. It treats criteria as independent, though they often interact. It turns qualitative judgements into numbers that look more precise than they are. It can be gamed by weighting the criteria to favour a preferred answer. And it cannot capture factors you forgot to include. Use it to structure your thinking, not to replace it. When the result surprises you, treat the surprise as information and ask what you know that the matrix does not.

Using the matrix for team decisions

In a team, agree on the criteria and their weights before anyone scores the options. Let each person score independently, then compare, since large differences point to disagreements about facts or priorities worth discussing. Average the scores or agree on a final set. Because the matrix is transparent, anyone who disagrees with the outcome can see exactly which weight or score to challenge. Save the matrix file and share it so that the reasoning behind the decision is recorded.

Decision matrix compared with a pros and cons list

A pros and cons list is fast and good for simple yes or no questions, but it treats every point as equal and handles only one option at a time. A decision matrix compares several options side by side, gives each factor a weight and ends in a number you can examine. Use a pros and cons list when you are deciding whether to do something, and a decision matrix when you are choosing between several alternatives or when stakeholders must see why one option won. For prioritising a long list of tasks rather than choosing between a few options, an Eisenhower matrix may be a better first step. Other tools such as cost-benefit analysis add money values for each factor, which suits business cases where costs and benefits can be measured in the same currency.

A worked example: choosing a job offer

Suppose you have three offers. Salary matters most to you, so it gets weight 9, followed by career growth at 8, work-life balance at 7, company stability at 6 and commute at 5. Commute is a lower-is-better criterion, so a short journey scores well. You score each offer from 0 to 10 for each criterion using facts where you can, such as pay figures and travel times. The calculator multiplies, adds and ranks the offers. In the built-in example, Offer A wins narrowly with 244 of a possible 350 points, but removing almost any single criterion changes the winner, so the robustness note warns that the choice is close. That tells you the numbers cannot settle it alone, and that it is worth talking to people, asking more questions or finding a criterion that separates the offers.

Common decision matrix mistakes

  • Too many criteria. Keep to the ones that really matter.
  • Overlapping criteria. Quality and reliability may count the same thing twice.
  • Forgetting to flip cost-like criteria. Use Lower is better.
  • Weighting after scoring. Set weights first.
  • Ignoring a close result. If the top two are close, look at other factors.
  • Scoring from feelings. Use data where possible.

Frequently asked questions

What is a decision matrix?

A table that compares options against several criteria. Each criterion has a weight for its importance, each option gets a score for each criterion, and the weighted scores are added to rank the options.

How do I use this free online decision matrix calculator?

Enter your options and criteria, give each criterion a weight, score every option and read the ranking. Change any number to see the result update instantly.

What is a weighted decision matrix?

A decision matrix in which criteria have different weights. A criterion with weight 10 counts twice as much as one with weight 5.

How is the total score calculated?

For each option, multiply the score for each criterion by that criterion's weight and add the results. The percent of maximum shows the total as a share of the best possible score.

Other names for a decision matrix?

It is also called a Pugh matrix, a weighted scoring model, a multi-criteria decision analysis tool, a decision grid, a prioritisation matrix or a selection matrix.

How do I choose weights?

Rate how important each criterion is from 1 to 10. A useful method is to rank the criteria first and then assign weights that reflect the order. Use Set all weights equal to see the result without weighting.

What score scale should I use?

A 0 to 10 scale gives more detail and 0 to 5 is quicker. Keep it consistent across options.

How do I handle costs, risks or effort?

Tick Lower is better for those criteria. The scale is flipped so that a low cost scores well, which avoids the common error of rewarding the most expensive option.

How many options and criteria can I add?

Up to 8 options and 12 criteria, which suits most decisions. Too many criteria dilute the important ones.

What does the robustness check do?

It removes each criterion in turn and checks whether the winner changes. If it does, that criterion decides the outcome and deserves a second look.

What if two options tie?

The tool tells you they are tied. Review your weights, add a criterion that separates them or choose with your intuition.

Is a decision matrix always right?

No. It makes your reasoning explicit and consistent, but it relies on your judgement of weights and scores. If the result feels wrong, find out why. A missing criterion or a mis-scored option is often the cause.

Can I download or print the result?

Yes. Download CSV for a spreadsheet, copy a text summary or print a clean sheet with the ranking and the full matrix.

Is my data saved?

Yes, in this browser on this device. Save the matrix file to keep a copy.

Can I use it for group decisions?

Yes. Agree on the criteria and weights together and let each person score the options, then compare results and discuss differences.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. The table scrolls sideways on small screens.

Is it free and private?

Yes. It is free to use online and your data stays in your browser.
