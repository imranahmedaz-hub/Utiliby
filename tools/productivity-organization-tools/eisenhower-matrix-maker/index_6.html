What this free online Eisenhower matrix maker does

Everything on your list seems to matter, and yet some things clearly matter more. The Eisenhower matrix, also called the urgent-important matrix or the Eisenhower box, helps you tell the difference. It asks two questions of every task and sorts it into one of four boxes, each with its own action: do it now, schedule it, delegate it or delete it. This free online Eisenhower matrix maker does the sorting for you, shows the result as a colour-coded diagram and gives feedback on how you are spending your attention.

Add tasks one at a time or paste a whole list. For each, say whether it is urgent and whether it is important, or pick a box yourself. Drag tasks between boxes as circumstances change, tick them off when done and download the finished matrix as an image, spreadsheet or text. The data stays in your browser, and there is no account.

How to use the Eisenhower matrix step by step

  1. Brain-dump. List everything you need to do, in any order.
  2. Ask two questions. Is this urgent? Is this important? Tick the boxes as you add each task.
  3. Check the result. The task appears in the right box with its action.
  4. Act. Do the first box today, schedule the second, delegate the third and delete the fourth.
  5. Review. Revisit the matrix daily or weekly and move tasks as things change.

4. EliminateNeitherDrop itTime-wasters, endless scrolling, busywork

The second box is the most valuable. These tasks never shout, so they are easy to ignore, yet they are the ones that move your life and career forward. People who feel permanently busy often have a very full first box because second-box tasks were put off until they became emergencies.

Where the Eisenhower matrix comes from

The method is named after Dwight D. Eisenhower, who was a general in the Second World War and later President of the United States. In a 1954 speech he quoted a university president who said, I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent. The principle became known as the Eisenhower principle. In 1989 Stephen R. Covey turned it into a four-quadrant grid in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People and urged readers to spend more of their time on important but not urgent work. The grid is now one of the most widely taught prioritisation tools.

How to decide what is urgent and important

Urgency is about time: does something bad happen soon if I do not act? Importance is about value: does this task serve a goal or a person that matters to me? A simple test for importance is to ask what would happen if you never did it. If the answer is nothing, it belongs in the lower boxes. A test for urgency is to ask who is waiting and what the real deadline is. A request marked urgent by someone else is not always urgent for you. Be honest, and remember that tasks move: a second-box task becomes first-box when the deadline arrives, which is why scheduling matters.

Tips for getting the most from the matrix

  • Limit the first box. If it holds more than five or six tasks, you are probably in firefighting mode.
  • Put dates on second-box tasks. Scheduled tasks are far likelier to happen.
  • Batch third-box tasks. Answer messages at set times.
  • Be ruthless with the fourth box. Delete what you will never do.
  • Use verbs. Write tasks as actions.
  • Re-sort weekly. Urgency changes as dates approach.

Who uses the Eisenhower matrix

Managers use it to separate crises from strategy and decide what to delegate. Students use it to balance deadlines with long-term study. Founders use it to protect time for product work from constant interruptions. Parents use it to separate real household emergencies from tasks that can wait. It also fits personal decisions such as health, finances and relationships, because the second box invites you to put what matters on the calendar. The matrix combines well with other tools: pick tasks here, then follow them on a Kanban board, schedule them in a weekly timetable or work through them with a Pomodoro timer.

Combining the matrix with other productivity tools

The matrix tells you what deserves your attention, but not how to carry it out. After sorting, many people copy the first-box tasks to a to-do list for today, place second-box tasks on a weekly schedule so they get a real time slot, and run focused work with a Pomodoro timer. A Kanban board is useful when several tasks are in progress at once and you want to see where each one stands. Meeting-heavy third-box tasks can be reduced with a clear agenda and a time limit. The point is to let each tool do one job: the matrix for deciding, the list or board for tracking, the calendar for timing and the timer for focus.

Revisit the matrix at the start of each week. Look at what stayed in the second box without a date, and give each one a time. Then look at the fourth box and delete anything you have been avoiding for weeks, because it is probably not worth the guilt it causes.

Common Eisenhower matrix mistakes

  • Treating everything as urgent and important. If every task lands in the first box, question your answers.
  • Never visiting the second box. Schedule one item from it every day.
  • Not delegating. Third-box tasks need a plan to hand off, batch or decline.
  • Confusing other people's urgency with your own.
  • Making it too elaborate. Keep tasks short and review often.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Eisenhower matrix?

A prioritisation tool with four boxes, built from two questions: is the task urgent and is it important? The answers tell you whether to do it now, schedule it, delegate it or eliminate it.

How do I use this free online Eisenhower matrix maker?

Type a task, answer whether it is urgent and important, and press Add task. It lands in the right box. Drag it to another box if you change your mind.

What is the difference between urgent and important?

Urgent tasks demand immediate attention and have a close deadline. Important tasks contribute to your long-term goals and values. Many urgent tasks are not important, and many important tasks are never urgent.

What goes in the Do first box?

Tasks that are both urgent and important, such as a deadline tomorrow or a real emergency. Handle them now.

What goes in the Schedule box?

Important but not urgent tasks such as planning, learning, exercise and relationships. Put a date and time on them.

What goes in the Delegate box?

Urgent but not important tasks, such as some requests, emails and calls. Ask someone else, automate them or limit the time they take.

What goes in the Eliminate box?

Tasks that are neither urgent nor important, like time-wasting habits and low-value busywork. Drop them.

Who created the Eisenhower matrix?

It is named after Dwight D. Eisenhower, who is credited with the principle that what is important is seldom urgent. Stephen Covey popularised the four-box version in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.
