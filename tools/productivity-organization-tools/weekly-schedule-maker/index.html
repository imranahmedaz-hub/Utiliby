What this free online weekly schedule maker does

A week has only 168 hours, and most of them are already spoken for by sleep, work or school, travel and meals. A written schedule shows what is left and lets you decide how to use it, instead of discovering on Friday that the week has gone. This free online weekly schedule maker turns your commitments into a clear timetable: days across the top, hours down the side and each activity as a coloured block.

It is useful for students planning classes and study, employees organising meetings and focus time, parents coordinating school runs and activities, freelancers blocking client work and anyone building an exercise or meal-prep routine. Add events once, repeat them across days, spot clashes at a glance and see how many hours you have booked each day. Download the result as an image or spreadsheet, or print it for the wall.

How to make a weekly schedule step by step

  1. Set the frame. Choose the hours your day covers and whether to include weekends.
  2. Add fixed commitments. Start with classes, work hours, appointments and regular events. Enter the title, start and end time and tick the days.
  3. Add goals. Block time for study, exercise, hobbies or deep work.
  4. Leave buffers. Keep gaps for travel, meals and surprises.
  5. Check clashes and totals. Dashed outlines show overlaps. The chart shows the hours per day.
  6. Save and share. Download a PNG, SVG or CSV or print it.

Time blocking: the method behind a good weekly plan

Time blocking means giving every important task a place on the calendar instead of leaving it on a list. Cal Newport, the author of Deep Work, popularised the idea that a planned day gets more meaningful work done than a reactive one. The steps are simple. First place the blocks that cannot move, such as classes, shifts and meetings. Second, place your most demanding work in the time of day when you have most energy. Third, group similar light tasks like email and admin into one block. Finally, leave deliberate gaps, because plans always take longer than expected.

A weekly view helps because it shows balance. If Tuesday has nine hours of meetings and Thursday has none, you can move something. If every evening is full, you can add an evening of rest.

Schedules for students and families

For students, a weekly timetable shows when classes run and when the gaps are available for revision. Colour each subject, add the room and place study blocks after lectures to review while the material is fresh. Many students find that a short, regular study block each day beats a long cram before an exam. For families, a shared print-out on the fridge reduces morning chaos: school hours, clubs, pick-ups, meals and chores all in one place. Choose one colour per person and use the notes for locations.

Schedules for work and freelancing

For office or remote work, mark recurring meetings first and protect two or three blocks a week for deep, uninterrupted work. Group meetings on one or two days so that other days stay clear. Freelancers can reserve client hours, admin time and a block for finding new work, which is often the first thing to be forgotten. Shift workers can lay out rotating shifts, with rest and commuting time. Remember that the schedule describes intent, so review it every week and adjust to what really happened.

Balancing work, study, health and rest

A schedule that fills every hour is not a plan; it is a source of stress. Include meals, travel, sleep routines, exercise and free time as real events. Adults are advised by health organisations to get regular physical activity and enough sleep, and a visible block makes both more likely. If the busiest-day figure is far above the others, spread the load. If you rarely finish what you plan, shorten the plan instead of working longer.

Tips for a schedule you will actually follow

  • Be realistic. Estimate with a margin.
  • Plan the night before. Look at tomorrow's blocks.
  • Use colour consistently. One colour per category.
  • Review weekly. Spend ten minutes on Sunday adjusting the next week.
  • Keep a buffer day. Leave one lighter period for overflow.
  • Save versions. Download the file before big changes.

A ten-minute weekly review

Once a week, look back at what actually happened before you plan the next week. Which blocks did you keep? Which did you move or skip, and why? Were there tasks that took twice as long as planned? Adjust durations, move difficult work to your best hours and remove blocks that you never follow. Then look ahead: add appointments, deadlines and travel, and make sure there is at least one block for something you enjoy. A short, regular review is more effective than a perfect plan that is never revised.

Common weekly planning mistakes

  • Overbooking. Do not schedule every hour.
  • Ignoring travel time. Add it as an event.
  • No time for rest or meals. Treat them as appointments.
  • Planning only tasks. Include goals as well as obligations.
  • Never revisiting the plan. Adapt it as life changes.

Privacy, limits and responsible use
