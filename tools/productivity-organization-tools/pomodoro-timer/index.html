What this free Pomodoro timer does

Focus is hard to hold for hours but easy to hold for twenty-five minutes. The Pomodoro Technique, created by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, turns that observation into a routine: work on one task for a short, fixed period, rest briefly, and repeat. A Pomodoro timer keeps the routine honest, because it tells you when to start, when to stop and when you have earned a longer break. This free online timer does that with a large progress ring, automatic switching between focus, short break and long break, custom lengths for every stage, a choice of sounds and optional notifications.

It also remembers your work. Each finished focus session is saved with a task label in your browser, and the statistics show today's sessions, the last seven days, your streak and your all-time totals, with a bar chart. The log can be downloaded as a CSV or printed. Nothing is uploaded, and there is no account.

How to use the Pomodoro timer

  1. Choose one task. Type it in the task box so that it is saved with the session.
  2. Check the lengths. Keep 25, 5 and 15 or choose a preset.
  3. Press Start. Work only on that task until the timer rings.
  4. Take the break. Stand up, drink water and rest your eyes. Avoid your phone if you can.
  5. Repeat. After the set number of focus sessions the timer offers a long break.
  6. Review your day. The statistics show how much focused time you really had.

The Pomodoro Technique step by step

The method has five rules. Decide on the task. Set the timer. Work until it rings, without switching to anything else. Take a short break. After four rounds, take a longer one. The power of the method comes from the limits: the timer is short enough that starting feels easy, and the break is a promise that rest is coming, which reduces the urge to check messages. If a distracting thought arrives, write it down and return to the task. If you finish early, use the remaining time to review the work instead of starting something new. If an interruption cannot wait, pause or reset the timer and start the session again afterwards.

Which timing should you use?

The classic 25 and 5 is a good start, but it is not the only choice. Longer blocks suit tasks that need time to warm up, such as writing, programming and design, while shorter ones suit tasks that you dread or that involve many small steps.

Try one for a week, note how you feel at the end of each block and adjust by five minutes at a time.

Pomodoro for students, writers and remote workers

Students can give each session one clear goal, such as read ten pages or solve five problems, and use breaks to stand up rather than to scroll. Writers often prefer longer blocks, since the first ten minutes go to getting back into the text. Programmers can use the task box to record the ticket they worked on and count how many sessions a task really takes, which makes estimates more realistic. People who work from home can use the long break as a hard stop for lunch or a walk. Teams sometimes run a shared Pomodoro by agreeing on a start time and silencing chat during the focus block.

Making it work when focus is hard

If you struggle to start, shorten the block until it feels almost too easy, for example ten minutes, and promise only to begin. Many people find that the hardest part is the first minute, and that they continue once they are in motion. Use auto-start for breaks and focus sessions if you tend to drift during the break. Keep your phone out of reach, close extra tabs and put the task in writing before you press Start. This tool is not a medical aid. If you have a condition that affects attention, a qualified professional can suggest approaches that suit you.

How to use breaks well

A break is only restful if it differs from work. Look away from the screen, stretch, walk, refill a glass of water, or look out of a window. Avoid social media and the news during a short break, because they pull attention to new topics and make the return harder. Use the long break for a meal, a short walk or a call with a friend. Good breaks protect your eyes, back and mood, which pays back in the next session.

Tracking your progress

The statistics help you notice patterns without a spreadsheet. Today shows sessions and minutes so far. The seven-day chart reveals which weekdays are your best for focus. The streak counts consecutive days with at least one completed session, which is a gentle way to build a habit. The log keeps the task label, so you can see where time went and use it in a weekly review. Download the CSV if you want to chart the data elsewhere, and clear the log when you want a fresh start.

Common Pomodoro mistakes

  • Working on several tasks in one session. Choose one.
  • Skipping breaks. Fatigue builds, and quality drops.
  • Using breaks for your phone. It does not rest the mind.
  • Setting blocks that are too long. If you often quit early, shorten them.
  • Ignoring interruptions. Pause, note them and return.
  • Counting unfinished sessions. Only completed ones count.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Pomodoro timer?

A timer for the Pomodoro Technique, a method that splits work into focused sessions, traditionally 25 minutes, separated by short breaks. After about four sessions you take a longer break.

How do I use this free Pomodoro timer online?

Type what you are working on, press Start and focus until the timer ends. Take the break when it rings. The timer moves to the next stage by itself, and you can choose whether it starts automatically.

What are the classic Pomodoro times?

25 minutes of focus, a 5-minute short break and a 15 to 30-minute long break after four sessions. This timer uses 25, 5 and 15 by default, and you can change every length.

Can I change the lengths?

Yes. Set any focus length from 1 to 240 minutes and any break length, or choose a preset such as 50 and 10, 52 and 17, or 90 and 20 for deep work.

Does the timer work if I switch tabs?

Yes. It follows the clock, so it stays accurate when the tab is in the background. The remaining time also shows in the browser tab title.

Will I hear a sound when the session ends?

Yes, if sound is on. Choose beep, bell or chime, set the volume and press Test sound. Some browsers block sound until you have interacted with the page, which pressing Start does.

Can it send notifications?

Yes. Tick the notification option and allow notifications when your browser asks. Notifications depend on your device settings.

Are my sessions saved?

Yes, in this browser only. Each finished focus session is added to a local log with the date, length and your task label. It is never uploaded.

What do the statistics show?

Sessions and minutes today, the last seven days, your streak of consecutive days and all-time totals, plus a bar chart of focus minutes per day.

Can I download my log?

Yes. Download it as a CSV for a spreadsheet, copy a summary or print it.

What are the keyboard shortcuts?

Space starts or pauses, R resets the current timer and S skips to the next stage when you are not typing in a field.

What if I get interrupted?

Pause the timer and resume afterwards, or reset it if the interruption was long. The technique treats an interrupted session as unfinished, so only completed sessions are logged.

How long should a focus session be?

25 minutes suits most people. Try 50 for deep work or 15 if you struggle to start. Choose a length that you can keep without drifting.

Is the Pomodoro Technique scientifically proven?

Structured breaks are supported by research on attention and fatigue, but the exact 25-minute length is a convention, not a law. Treat it as a tool and adjust it to your work.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. It adapts to small screens. Keep the screen on or the page open for sounds, as mobile browsers may pause pages in the background.

Is it free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up.

Is my data private?

Yes. Everything stays in your browser, and nothing is sent to a server.
