What does my IP address reveal about me?

Your public IP address reveals more than most people realise:

  • Approximate location. Usually city-level accuracy — enough to know your region and often your neighbourhood, but not your street address.
  • Internet Service Provider (ISP). The company that provides your internet connection.
  • Autonomous System Number (ASN). The identifier for the network block your IP belongs to.
  • Organization. The company or entity that owns the IP block — often your ISP, but sometimes a hosting provider, university or business.
  • Country and region. Your general geographic area.
  • Timezone. Your local timezone, which can be inferred from your location.

Your IP does not reveal your name, email, exact address, phone number or personal identity on its own. But combined with other data — browser fingerprint, cookies, login sessions — it can contribute to uniquely identifying you.

IPv4 vs IPv6: what is the difference?

IPv4 is the older addressing system, using 32-bit addresses that look like 192.168.1.1. It supports about 4.3 billion addresses, and they ran out years ago. IPv6 is the newer system, using 128-bit addresses that look like 2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334. It supports an effectively unlimited number of addresses.

Most devices today have both. Your ISP may prefer one over the other depending on how your network is configured. If you see an IPv6 address but not an IPv4 address (or vice versa), that is simply the protocol your device prefers for this connection.

How a VPN hides your IP address

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN server. All your internet traffic goes through this tunnel and exits from the VPN server's IP address. Websites you visit see the VPN server's IP, not yours. Your real IP is hidden from them entirely.

The VPN also encrypts your traffic, which prevents your ISP from seeing what you do online. However, a VPN does not make you anonymous — the VPN provider itself can see your traffic, and the websites you log into still know who you are if you are signed in.

Proxy vs VPN: what is the difference?

A proxy server reroutes your traffic through an intermediate server. Unlike a VPN, a proxy typically does not encrypt your traffic — it just changes the visible IP address. Proxies are often application-specific (a browser proxy, an HTTP proxy) rather than system-wide.

For privacy purposes, VPNs are generally stronger than proxies because they encrypt everything. But both hide your IP, and both can be detected by sophisticated websites that check for known proxy and VPN IP ranges.

What is a WebRTC leak?

WebRTC (Web Real-Time Communication) is a browser technology that enables video calls, voice chat and peer-to-peer file sharing. It uses ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment) candidates to find the best network path between two devices. Those candidates can contain your local IP address and, in some configurations, your real public IP address — even when you are connected to a VPN.

This is called a WebRTC leak. It is a well-known privacy issue that can defeat the IP-hiding purpose of a VPN. The leak happens because WebRTC traffic may bypass the VPN tunnel entirely, using your real network interface.

How to fix a WebRTC leak:

  • Use a VPN with built-in WebRTC leak protection.
  • Disable WebRTC in your browser (via extensions like WebRTC Leak Prevent).
  • Use a browser that routes WebRTC through the VPN tunnel.
  • Configure your firewall to block WebRTC traffic outside the VPN.

What is a browser fingerprint?

A browser fingerprint is a set of characteristics that can uniquely identify your browser and device. Unlike cookies, fingerprints cannot easily be cleared — they are based on stable properties of your system:

  • Screen resolution and colour depth
  • Timezone and language settings
  • Installed fonts
  • User agent (browser and OS details)
  • Hardware capabilities (CPU cores, memory, GPU)
  • Canvas and WebGL rendering
  • Audio processing
  • Do Not Track and other preferences

The combination of these signals creates a hash that is often unique enough to track you across websites, even in incognito mode. This tool displays what your browser is revealing.

What is a Tor exit node?

Tor (The Onion Router) routes your traffic through multiple volunteer-run servers, encrypting it in layers at each hop. The last server in the chain — the exit node — is what websites see. Tor provides strong anonymity but is slower than a VPN and often blocked by websites that flag Tor exit node IPs.

This tool checks whether your IP is a known Tor exit node, a known VPN server, or a known proxy. If any of those are true, the security badge will indicate it.

Why does IP privacy matter?

Your IP address is tied to your internet connection, which in most countries is registered to a physical address and a billing account. Your ISP can see every site you connect to (though not the content if you use HTTPS). Websites can see your approximate location and use it for geo-targeting, ad personalisation and sometimes content restriction.

For most people, this level of exposure is acceptable. For journalists, activists, researchers, remote workers and privacy-conscious users, hiding the IP is a legitimate and often necessary step. A VPN, Tor or proxy provides that protection.
