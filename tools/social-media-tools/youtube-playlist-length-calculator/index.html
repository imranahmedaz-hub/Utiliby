What a YouTube playlist length calculator does

A long playlist is a commitment. A programming course may have 80 videos, a lecture series 40 hours and a music mix more than a day of audio. Before you start, you want to know one thing: how long will it take? A YouTube playlist length calculator answers that, and goes on to turn the total into a plan you can follow.

This free online tool adds the length of every video you list, shows the total at normal speed and at every playback speed from 0.5x to 2x, reports the average, longest and shortest video, and draws a chart of the lengths. Then it builds a day-by-day schedule from the minutes you can spare and the days you choose, shows the date you will finish and lets you download a CSV, add the plan to your calendar or print it. It works in your browser and sends nothing anywhere.

How to use the calculator

  1. List the lengths. Paste one video per line. Copy each length from the badge on the thumbnail. A title is optional.
  2. Or estimate. If you only know the count and an average length, use the Quick estimate tab.
  3. Pick a playback speed. See the total change at once, and compare every speed in the table.
  4. Set your study plan. Choose minutes per day, the days you can watch and the start date.
  5. Read the schedule. See which videos go on which day and the finish date.
  6. Save it. Copy the summary, download the CSV or calendar file or print the plan.

Playback speed and time saved

YouTube's player lets you change speed, and many learners watch lectures faster. The arithmetic is simple. The total time at a given speed is the normal total divided by the speed, so 1.5x finishes in two-thirds of the time and 2x in half. The table in the tool lists every speed for your playlist, with the time saved. Slower speeds, such as 0.75x, take longer, which can help with dense or technical material or with a language you are learning.

Building a realistic study schedule

Finishing a course is mostly about consistency. The schedule tool takes the minutes you can watch each day and fits whole videos into each day. If a day has 60 minutes and the next video would push it over, that video moves to the next day. A video longer than your daily allowance gets a day to itself. Untick the days you cannot study, and the plan skips them, so a weekday-only routine finishes later than a daily one, and the date tells you by how much.

  • Start small. A plan you keep at 30 minutes a day beats one you abandon at 3 hours.
  • Leave room for notes. Add 20 to 50 percent to the daily minutes if you pause to take notes or do exercises.
  • Build in a catch-up day. Leave one weekly slot free.
  • Put it in your calendar. The downloaded .ics file adds all days at once.

Worked example: an 8-video course

The default example lists eight videos totalling 2 hours 8 minutes at normal speed. At 1.5x the same course takes about 1 hour 25 minutes. With 60 minutes a day, the whole-video rule fits it into two days, and the calculator shows exactly which videos go on which day and the finish date. Change the speed to 1x and it takes three days. Then cut the daily minutes to 30, and it spreads over six. Each change updates the schedule instantly, so you can find a rhythm that fits your week.

When you do not have every length: the quick estimate

Some playlists are long, and typing every length is a chore. If the videos are similar, estimate. Enter the number of videos and a typical length, and the tool multiplies them. A 100-video playlist at 10 minutes each is 1,000 minutes, or 16 hours 40 minutes. The schedule then divides that time across your study days. Spot-check a handful of videos to choose a good average, and remember that a few very long videos can push the real total above the estimate.

Other ways to use it

  • Online courses. Plan a whole course before you enrol.
  • University lectures. Fit a recorded series into the weeks before an exam.
  • Language learning. Schedule a daily listening habit.
  • Music and podcasts. Find out how long a mix or a series lasts.
  • Watch parties and binge plans. Work out how long a season will take.
  • Content planning. Total up your own series for a playlist description.

Tips for finishing what you start

  • Watch actively. Pause and try what you see.
  • Use chapters. Skip to the part you need when reviewing.
  • Track progress. Update the number of videos watched and see the remaining time shrink.
  • Reduce friction. Same time, same place each day.
  • Stop when the goal is met. You do not have to watch everything in a long playlist if you have learned what you came for.

Privacy, accuracy and responsible use

The calculator runs in your browser and stores nothing. It does not connect to YouTube, so it cannot check a playlist for you: the total is only as accurate as the lengths you enter. Videos can be added, removed or edited in a playlist after you copy the lengths. Respect creators by watching on YouTube where possible, and treat the study plan as a suggestion that you can change at any time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a YouTube playlist length calculator?

A tool that adds up the lengths of the videos in a playlist or course and shows the total time, the time at different playback speeds and a schedule for finishing it.

How do I find the length of a YouTube playlist?

Open the playlist, copy each video's length from the thumbnail badge into this calculator, one per line, and read the total. For a playlist with many equal videos, use the Quick estimate tab with the count and an average length.

Can the tool read a playlist link?

No. It works in your browser with the lengths you enter and does not connect to YouTube. That also means nothing is sent anywhere.

What formats can I paste?

m:ss, h:mm:ss, and forms like 1h 5m or 45 min. A title before or after the time is allowed.

How much time do I save at 1.5x or 2x speed?

Total time is divided by the speed. At 1.5x you finish in two-thirds of the time and at 2x in half. The table shows every speed from 0.5x to 2x.

Is watching at faster speed effective?

Many learners use 1.25x to 1.5x for familiar material and slow down for new or difficult topics. Comprehension depends on the content and on you.

How do I plan a course to finish by a date?

Enter the minutes per day, pick your study days and the start date. The tool shows the day-by-day schedule and the date you finish. Increase the daily minutes to finish sooner.

Does the schedule split a video across two days?

No. It fits whole videos into each day's limit. A single video longer than the limit gets a day of its own.

Can I skip videos I have already watched?

Yes. Enter the number of videos already watched in list mode, and the remaining time and the schedule start from the next one.

Can I add the plan to my calendar?

Yes. Download the calendar file (.ics) and open it in Google Calendar, Apple Calendar or Outlook to get one all-day event per study day.

Can I choose which days of the week to study?

Yes. Untick the days you will not watch, for example weekends, and the schedule skips them.

What is the quick estimate for?

When you do not have every length. Enter the number of videos and an average length, and the tool multiplies them to give a total and a schedule.

How long is a 100-video playlist?

It depends on the lengths. At an average of 10 minutes, 100 videos take about 16 hours 40 minutes at normal speed, and about 11 hours 7 minutes at 1.5x.

Does it include time for notes and breaks?

No. It counts only video time. Add extra minutes to your daily allowance if you pause to take notes or do exercises.

Is my data uploaded?

No. The calculator runs in your browser and stores nothing.

Is this calculator free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up, and you can copy a summary, download a CSV or calendar file or print the plan.

Does it work for courses, podcasts and lecture series?

Yes. Any list of video or audio durations can be added up, including university lectures and online course videos.
