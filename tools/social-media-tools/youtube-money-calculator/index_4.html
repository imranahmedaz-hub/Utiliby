What this free YouTube money calculator does

The YouTube money calculator answers a question every creator asks: how much could my channel earn? You enter your views per day, week, month or year, choose a niche, tell the tool where your audience lives and how long your videos are, and it estimates your earnings in a range from low to high. It handles YouTube Shorts separately, because they pay much less per view, and lets you add sponsors, affiliate income and channel memberships to see your total.

It also includes two tools that most calculators lack. The goal planner works backward from the income you want and tells you how many views you need each month at low, typical and high RPM. The Partner Program check shows your progress toward the subscriber, watch hour and Shorts view requirements for monetization. The whole page works in your browser, and you can print or save the result as a CSV file.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter your views. Choose per day, week, month or year.
  2. Pick your niche. This sets the range of revenue per 1,000 views.
  3. Choose your audience and video length. Both change the estimate.
  4. Add Shorts if you post them. Enter the share of your views that come from Shorts.
  5. Open the optional section. Add your real RPM from YouTube Studio, plus sponsors, affiliates and memberships.
  6. Read the results. The table shows low, typical and high earnings for a day, a week, a month and a year.

RPM, CPM and how YouTube pays creators

Two terms matter. CPM is the cost per thousand ad views that advertisers pay. It applies only to views that show an ad, and YouTube takes a share, so you never receive the full CPM. RPM is revenue per thousand video views to you, across all views, including those that show no ad. RPM is the figure in YouTube Studio and the one this calculator uses. Because many views do not show an ad, and YouTube keeps a share of ad revenue, RPM is lower than CPM, often by half.

Long videos and Shorts are paid differently. Long videos earn from ads shown before, during and after them. Shorts earn from a pool of ad revenue shared among creators according to views, so each Shorts view is worth a fraction of a long-video view. Revenue is also paid only on watched, monetized views by eligible channels in the Partner Program, and payouts are subject to tax rules in your country.

What changes how much a channel earns

  • Niche and advertiser demand. Topics that attract high-value advertisers pay more.
  • Audience location. Some countries have far higher ad rates.
  • Audience age and interests. Viewers with more spending power are worth more to advertisers.
  • Season. Ad rates tend to rise in the last quarter of the year and fall in January.
  • Video length and ad placement. Videos over 8 minutes can carry mid-roll ads.
  • Advertiser-friendly content. Videos with limited ads earn less.
  • Watch time and retention. Videos that people watch longer are recommended more, which increases views.
  • Ad blockers and Premium. Views without ads do not earn ad revenue, though Premium viewers generate some revenue.

Shorts earnings in perspective

Shorts are excellent for reaching new viewers and can drive subscribers, but they earn little per view. The calculator uses 1 to 6 cents per 1,000 Shorts views. A million Shorts views at these rates produces between 10 and 60 dollars, compared with hundreds or thousands from a million long-video views. Many creators use Shorts to grow an audience and earn mainly from long videos, memberships, sponsorships and their own products. Enter your Shorts share and watch how the total changes.

Other ways channels earn money

  • Sponsorships. A brand pays you to feature a product. Fees often depend on views and audience, not just subscribers.
  • Affiliate links. You earn a commission when viewers buy through your link.
  • Channel memberships. Viewers pay a monthly fee, and YouTube keeps about 30%.
  • Merchandise and products. Digital goods, courses and physical items can outearn ads for small channels.
  • Super Chat and Thanks. Viewer payments during live streams and on videos.

Sponsored content must be disclosed in most countries. Use YouTube's paid promotion label and follow the advertising rules where you live.

Planning backward from an income goal

The goal planner turns a monthly income target into the views you need. It subtracts the other income you entered, divides what is left by the RPM for each scenario and multiplies by 1,000. A creator who wants 3,000 dollars a month from ads at a 6 dollar RPM needs 500,000 views a month. At a 3 dollar RPM the same goal needs a million. Seeing the range helps you decide whether to grow views, move into a higher RPM niche, add sponsors or build a product, and it shows how realistic a target is compared with your current numbers.

YouTube Partner Program requirements

To earn from ads, a channel has to join the Partner Program. YouTube has published a full tier that asks for 1,000 subscribers and either 4,000 public watch hours in the last 12 months or 10 million public Shorts views in the last 90 days. An early tier, which unlocks memberships and fan funding, has asked for 500 subscribers, three public uploads in the last 90 days, and either 3,000 watch hours or 3 million Shorts views. Channels must also follow the monetization policies, live in an eligible country and have an AdSense account. Rules change, so check YouTube Studio for yours.

How accurate is the estimate?

Treat the numbers as a planning guide. The calculator does not know your real ad rates, audience age, seasonality, advertiser-friendly status, ad blocker share or which videos carry ads. Two channels with the same views can earn very different amounts. The most accurate input is your own RPM from YouTube Studio, which you can type in under the optional section. For a new channel without history, the low column is a safer planning number than the high one.

Privacy, limits and responsible use

The calculator runs in your browser. The numbers you enter are not sent or stored, and the print and CSV options contain only what is on screen. Estimates are not financial, tax or business advice, and no one can guarantee earnings. Do not buy views, subscribers or watch time to reach monetization thresholds, since that breaks YouTube's rules and can end your channel. Declare income to the tax authority in your country as required.

Frequently asked questions

How much does YouTube pay per 1,000 views?

It varies a lot. After YouTube's share, most creators see roughly 1 to 10 dollars per 1,000 views, with gaming and entertainment at the low end and finance and business at the high end. Your own RPM in YouTube Studio is the number that counts.

How does this YouTube money calculator work?

It multiplies your views by a range of revenue per 1,000 views (RPM) for your niche, adjusts it for where your viewers live and how long your videos are, treats Shorts separately and adds your sponsor, affiliate and membership income.

What is the difference between RPM and CPM?

CPM is what advertisers pay per 1,000 ad views, before YouTube's cut and only on monetized plays. RPM is what you earn per 1,000 total video views after YouTube takes its share, so RPM is always lower than CPM.

What is a good RPM on YouTube?

That depends on the niche. A few dollars is typical for many topics, and 10 dollars or more is common for finance, business and software. Shorts pay far less per view.

Can I enter my own RPM?

Yes. Open Other income and your own RPM, and type the figure from YouTube Studio. The calculator then uses it, with a range of 20% below and above, instead of the niche estimate.

How much do YouTube Shorts pay?

Much less per view than long videos, often a few cents per 1,000 views. The calculator uses 1 to 6 cents per 1,000 Shorts views, so a Shorts-heavy channel earns less from ads.

How many views do I need to make 1,000 dollars a month?

Use the Goal planner. At a 4 dollar RPM you need about 250,000 views a month. At 10 dollars you need 100,000, and at 1 dollar you need a million.

What do I need to join the YouTube Partner Program?

The full tier asks for 1,000 subscribers and either 4,000 public watch hours in 12 months or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days. An early tier asks for 500 subscribers and lower activity. Check the Partner Program tab and YouTube Studio for current rules.

Why does my niche matter so much?

Advertisers pay more to reach audiences who buy expensive things, such as financial products and software, so those videos earn a higher CPM and a higher RPM.

Does my audience location change earnings?

Yes. Viewers in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia usually bring higher ad rates than viewers in many other regions.

Do longer videos earn more?

Often yes. Videos over 8 minutes can include mid-roll ads, which add revenue. The calculator applies a small adjustment for length, but very long videos are not always better for viewers.

Are sponsors, affiliates and memberships included?

You can add them in the optional section. Memberships are reduced by YouTube's 30% share. Sponsor and affiliate income are counted in full.

Why is the range so wide?

Revenue differs from month to month and from channel to channel, even in the same niche. The low, typical and high columns show a realistic spread rather than one fixed number.

Is the estimate accurate?

It is a rough guide, not a forecast. It cannot see your real ad rates, season, audience age, ad blocker use or advertiser-friendly status. Use it for planning, and rely on YouTube Studio for real figures.

Does YouTube earn more in the last quarter of the year?

Yes. Ad demand usually rises in the fourth quarter because of holiday advertising, and it often falls in January. Real RPM can swing by season, which the calculator does not model.

Is my information stored or sent anywhere?

No. The calculation runs in your browser. Nothing is saved or uploaded.

Is it free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up.
