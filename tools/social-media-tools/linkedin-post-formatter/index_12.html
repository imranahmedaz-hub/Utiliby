What this free LinkedIn post formatter does

LinkedIn is a feed of text, and the way a post looks affects whether people read it. Short lines, clear spacing and one visual anchor make a post easy to scan on a phone. The platform's editor offers very little in the way of formatting, so writers use a LinkedIn post formatter to add bold and italic text, bullets and dividers. This free online tool does that and more: it keeps a live count against the 3,000-character limit, shows a see-more preview for desktop and mobile, flags a long first line or too many hashtags and offers six post templates and ten opening lines.

Formatting happens in your browser, with no sign-up and no upload. The tool gives you what you need to design a post, and it is honest about the limits of styled text, which can be hard for screen readers and for search.

How to use the formatter

  1. Write your post. Type it or paste it into the editor.
  2. Style key words. Select a phrase and click Bold, Italic, Sans, Mono, Underline or Strike. Use Plain to remove a style.
  3. Make lists. Select lines and click Bullets, Checks, Arrows or Numbers.
  4. Add structure. Insert a divider, and use Tidy spacing to clean up extra blank lines.
  5. Check the preview. See where see more cuts the post on desktop and on mobile.
  6. Copy and paste. Paste into the LinkedIn post box and check the result before you publish.

The first lines decide everything

On LinkedIn, the feed shows only the opening of a long post, followed by a see-more link. Readers decide in a second whether to expand. This makes the first one or two lines the most valuable space you have. Good openings state a result, a mistake, a surprising fact or a short story moment, and they promise something that the rest of the post delivers. Avoid starting with a greeting, an apology or a long introduction. The Templates tab has ten hooks, such as I was wrong about your topic and Unpopular opinion about your topic, that you can adapt to your own subject.

Layout that is easy to read

  • Short lines. One sentence or less per line, with a blank line between groups of ideas.
  • One idea. A post that tries to do three things usually does none well.
  • A list for steps. Numbers and bullets break up text and make takeaways easy to save.
  • A closing question. A real question invites comments, which help the post spread.
  • Hashtags at the end. Three or fewer, in camel case.

How styled text works, and its limits

Bold and italic on LinkedIn are not formatting at all. They are characters from the Unicode mathematical alphanumeric blocks, which look like styled letters. This is why they can be pasted anywhere. It is also why they have limits. A screen reader may read each letter as a mathematical symbol or skip it. Search does not match them to ordinary words. Some older devices show blank boxes. Use styled text for a single phrase or a heading, not whole paragraphs, and keep any word that matters for discovery in normal letters.

Bullets, numbers and dividers

Lists are the easiest way to make a post skimmable. Use bullets for parallel ideas, numbers for steps or ranked points, check marks for results and arrows for cause and effect. Keep each item to one line. A divider line, like a row of horizontal bars, separates sections in a longer post, but too many look heavy, so use one or two at most. The Tidy spacing button removes trailing spaces and more than one blank line in a row, so your layout is consistent.

Hashtags, mentions and links

A few relevant hashtags help LinkedIn understand the topic and let people follow it. Three or fewer is a common recommendation. Write them in camel case, so each word is clear to a screen reader, and keep them at the end of the post. Tag people only when it is relevant to them and they would want to know. If you share a link, many creators put it in the first comment because posts with outside links can reach fewer people, though results vary and the platform changes how it distributes posts.

Accessibility and professionalism

A post that looks clever to you can be unreadable to someone using assistive technology. Use plain language, avoid walls of emoji, describe images in alt text and do not hide meaning in styled characters. Remember that a LinkedIn audience is made of colleagues, clients and recruiters. Keep the tone friendly and honest, and avoid exaggerating results or claiming credit that belongs to others.

Privacy, accuracy and responsible use

The formatter runs in your browser, and your draft is not stored or sent. The templates are general, so replace them with real experience. Do not share confidential information about an employer, a client or a colleague, and check that you have permission to name people and companies. Follow LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies, disclose sponsored content and respect copyright for images and quotations. The preview is an approximation, and LinkedIn may change limits, truncation and design at any time.

Post types and rhythm on LinkedIn

Text posts are the simplest format, and the one this formatter is made for. Document posts, which show a set of slides that readers swipe through, suit step-by-step guides and checklists. Polls invite quick answers and work best with a clear question and two to four honest options. Short videos suit stories and demonstrations, and newsletters suit longer ideas that deserve an archive. Match the format to the message: a lesson with three points fits a text post, while a process with ten steps fits a document.

Rhythm matters as much as format. Most professionals do well with two to four posts a week, at times when their audience is at work, such as mornings on weekdays. Reply to comments in the first hour, because early conversation helps a post reach more people, and comment on other people's posts so that your name is seen in the feed. A consistent voice and a clear topic over months build far more recognition than an occasional viral post. Frequently asked questions

What is a LinkedIn post formatter?

A tool that helps you lay out a LinkedIn post with bold or italic text, bullets, dividers and clean spacing. This free one also previews the see-more cut-off and counts characters against the 3,000 limit.

How do I use this free LinkedIn post formatter online?

Write or paste your post, select some text and click Bold, Italic or another style. Use the list buttons for bullets or numbers, check the preview, then press Copy and paste the post into LinkedIn.

Does LinkedIn support bold and italic text?

Not natively in normal posts. Formatters use special Unicode characters that look like bold or italic, which you paste into the post. They can look different on some devices.

Are styled characters safe to use?

In small doses. Screen readers may read each styled letter by name or skip it, the text is not searchable and some devices show boxes. Use styling for a few words and keep important words in plain text.

How many characters can a LinkedIn post have?

As we understand current rules, 3,000 characters for a post. The counter shows how many you have used and warns you when you are close.

What is see more and how many characters show before it?

LinkedIn truncates long posts and shows a see more link. About 210 characters show on desktop and about 140 on mobile, though it varies. Put the hook first.

How do I write a strong first line?

Make a clear promise, a surprising fact or a short personal moment, in 60 characters or fewer if you can. The Templates tab lists ten opening lines you can adapt.

Why are my blank lines disappearing?

LinkedIn can collapse extra blank lines in some situations. The option Keep blank lines adds an invisible spacer character on empty lines when you copy to help preserve spacing.

How many hashtags should I use?

Three or fewer is common advice. Place them at the end, use camel case such as LeadershipTips for readability and choose tags that match the topic.

How long should a LinkedIn post be?

Short posts of 600 to 1,300 characters are common, but length matters less than value. Break the text into short lines and one idea per post.

Can I use emoji and bullets?

Yes, sparingly. A few symbols help the eye, but many emoji look cluttered and are read aloud by screen readers.

Are templates safe to use as written?

They are starting points. Replace the example details with your own, and do not claim results or stories that are not yours.

Will the formatting work in comments and in my headline?

Unicode styles generally paste into comments and profile fields too, but some fields may restrict them, and the same accessibility concerns apply. Test before relying on it.

Can I undo a style?

Select the text and click Plain, or press Undo to step back through recent changes.

Is my text uploaded?

No. Everything stays in your browser.

Is it free?

Yes. It is free to use online and needs no account.

Will the post look exactly like the preview?

The preview is an approximation. LinkedIn changes its design, fonts and truncation, so check the final post in the app before you publish.
