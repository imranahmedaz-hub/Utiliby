What this free online refinance loan term calculator does

When you refinance, the interest rate is only half of the decision. The other half is the term: how many years you give yourself to repay. A 15-year loan, a 20-year loan and a 30-year loan can all be quoted on the same day, each with a different rate, a different payment and a very different total cost. Most calculators compare one new loan with the old one. This free online refinance loan term calculator compares five terms at once, each with its own rate, against your current loan.

For every term it shows the new payment, how much the payment changes, the date you would be debt free, the total interest and costs, the amount saved against keeping your current loan and the break-even month. It also handles a common situation that simple tools ignore: an adjustable-rate loan that is about to reset. Everything runs in your browser, so your figures stay private.

How to use the refinance loan term calculator

  1. Enter your current loan. Use the balance, the rate, and the years left. If the loan is adjustable, change the loan type and enter the months until it resets and the rate you expect after the reset.
  2. Add the costs. Enter the refinance costs as a percentage of the new loan, and choose whether you pay them at closing or add them to the loan.
  3. Enter the quoted rates. Use the rate you were offered for each term. If you have not been quoted one, leave the example and treat the result as a guide only.
  4. Choose the term to analyse. The Results tab explains that term in detail, the All terms tab compares all five and the Balance tab shows how the balance falls.
  5. Change one thing at a time. Try a different term, a different reset rate or a different stay to see what drives the answer.

The trade-off between payment and total cost

A loan payment has two jobs: it pays the interest that has built up, and it reduces the balance. A shorter term forces more of each payment into the balance, so the balance falls quickly and less interest builds up. The price is a higher monthly payment. A longer term does the reverse. The payment is easier but the balance falls slowly, so you pay interest on a large balance for years.

Rates add a second effect. Lenders usually charge less for shorter terms, because they lend the money for a shorter time. So moving to a shorter term often cuts total interest twice: once because the rate is lower and again because the debt is repaid sooner. The result can be surprising. On a balance of 300,000, moving from a 6.75% loan with 28 years left to a 15-year loan at 5.75% raises the payment, yet the lifetime interest and costs fall by well over a hundred thousand.

Worked example

A homeowner owes 300,000 at 6.75% with 28 years left. The payment is about 1,990 a month. A 30-year loan at 6.50% would lower the payment to about 1,900, but it restarts the clock, so the total interest is still high. A 15-year loan at 5.75% would raise the payment to about 2,490, roughly 500 more each month, and clear the debt 13 years sooner. With costs of 2.5% of the loan paid at closing, the 15-year loan saves a large amount of interest and the costs are repaid within the first few years. The tool shows each of these figures for your own numbers.

The point is not that the shortest term always wins. It is that the answer depends on what you can pay. If the 15-year payment would crowd out savings or leave no cushion for repairs, a 20-year or 25-year loan may give most of the benefit with less strain.

Refinancing an adjustable-rate loan before it resets

An adjustable-rate mortgage starts with a fixed introductory rate, then changes to a rate based on an index plus a margin, within caps. If rates have risen, the payment can jump at the first reset. Many borrowers refinance into a fixed rate before that happens. The comparison is not a fair fight if you only look at the starting payment, because the ARM is cheap at first and expensive later.

Choose the adjustable loan type, enter the months until it resets and the rate you expect afterwards, and the calculator follows the ARM through the reset. The new fixed loans are compared with that path, so the savings reflect what the ARM would really cost. The reset rate is an assumption, and it is worth trying a few values. Your loan documents show the index, margin and caps that apply to you.

Conventional, jumbo and other loans

The tool works on principal and interest, so it can be used for any fixed-rate loan. Jumbo loans, which are above conforming limits, can be priced differently for different terms, and lenders may charge more fees or ask for larger reserves. That is why the rate for each term is an input rather than a rule of thumb. For government-backed loans with mortgage insurance, the streamline calculator on this site adds the insurance.

How to choose a term

  • Start with the payment you can sustain. Test the budget with the new payment, not only today's.
  • Keep an emergency fund. A very short term can leave you house-rich and cash-poor.
  • Consider your age and plans. Many people aim to be debt free by a target date such as retirement.
  • Compare with extra payments. A longer loan with voluntary extra payments gives you a safety valve.
  • Check for penalties. Some loans charge for early repayment. Ask before you assume you can prepay.

Common mistakes

  • Resetting the clock without noticing. A new 30-year loan after years of payments can cost more than the old loan.
  • Comparing only payments. The cheapest payment is not the cheapest loan.
  • Ignoring costs. Fees reduce the saving and move the break-even later.
  • Using the starting payment of an ARM. Compare with the payment after the reset.
  • Assuming the example rates are yours. Replace every rate with a real quote.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best term for a refinance?

There is no single best term. A shorter term saves interest and clears the debt sooner but raises the payment. A longer term lowers the payment but costs more in total. The best term is the shortest one whose payment you can keep up comfortably, with room for other goals.

Is a 15-year refinance better than a 30-year?

It is cheaper overall because the rate is usually lower and you pay interest for half as long. The payment is much higher, though. If the higher payment strains your budget, a 30-year loan with extra payments when you can afford them gives you flexibility.

How much interest can I save by shortening my term?

It depends on your balance, rate and the rates offered, but the saving is often large. Enter the rates you were quoted and the All terms tab shows the interest and costs for every term against keeping your current loan.

Will my payment go up if I refinance to a shorter term?

Usually yes, unless your current rate is much higher than the new one. The monthly change tile shows whether the payment rises or falls, and by how much.

Should I refinance from an ARM to a fixed rate?

Many people do so before the first reset to avoid a possible payment jump. Choose the adjustable loan type, enter the months until it resets and the rate you expect afterwards, and the tool compares that path with fixed-rate terms. The reset rate is your assumption, so try several.

How does the ARM option work in this calculator?

It follows your current loan at its starting rate until the reset month, then recalculates the payment on the remaining balance at the reset rate for the remaining term. That is a single reset. Real loans can adjust more than once under caps.

What does a longer term do to my total cost?

It spreads the balance over more years, so you pay interest for longer. Even at the same rate, a longer term costs more in total. Starting a new 30-year clock after several years of payments can also extend your debt-free date.

What is the break-even on a term change?

It is the month when the interest you save has repaid the refinance costs. When the payment rises, the saving shows up as less interest over time, not a lower bill, so the tool counts interest rather than payments.

Should I roll the costs into the loan?

It avoids cash at closing but raises the balance, so you pay interest on the costs. It can make sense when cash is tight. The tool shows both choices, so you can compare.

Can I use this for a jumbo loan?

Yes. Enter your balance and the rates you were quoted. Jumbo and conventional loans can be priced differently by term, so use real quotes for each.

Does a shorter term mean a lower rate?

Often, but not always. Lenders usually price shorter terms lower, though the difference changes with the market and the lender. That is why the tool asks for a rate for each term rather than assuming a gap.

How do extra payments compare with a shorter term?

Paying extra on a 30-year loan can mimic a shorter term while keeping the option to pay less in a hard month. The rate on a true 15-year loan is usually lower, so it normally saves more interest for the same payment.

How long should I stay to make a refinance worth it?

At least until the break-even month. Enter how long you expect to stay and the Saved by year tile shows the interest saved less costs at that point.

Does the calculator include taxes, insurance and mortgage insurance?

No. It compares principal and interest only, because taxes and insurance are usually the same whichever term you pick. If mortgage insurance is part of your payment, see the other refinance calculators on this site.

How accurate are the default figures?

They are only examples. Replace the balance, rate and the quoted rates for each term with your own numbers, and use a lender's Loan Estimate for the final decision.

Is my information private?

Yes. The calculator runs in your browser. Nothing you enter is sent to a server, saved or shared, and reloading the page resets it.
