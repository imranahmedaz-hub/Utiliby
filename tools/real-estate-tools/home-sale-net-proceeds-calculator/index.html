What a home sale net proceeds calculator does

The price on a sale contract is not the money you receive. A home sale net proceeds calculator (also called a seller net sheet) estimates what is left after agent commission, closing costs, transfer taxes, repairs, concessions, property tax proration and the payoff of your mortgage. Knowing that number before you list tells you whether you can afford to move, how much your next down payment can be and how low an offer you can accept.

This free online calculator gives an itemised statement and a waterfall chart, a table of commission and price scenarios, a reverse calculation that finds the sale price you need to reach a target amount of cash, and an optional capital gains estimate using the United States home-sale exclusion. It runs entirely in your browser.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter the expected sale price and your payoff. Get the payoff amount from your lender, since it includes interest to the closing date.
  2. Add commission and closing costs. Use your listing agreement and local transfer tax rates.
  3. Add repairs, staging and concessions. Include everything you expect to spend or credit.
  4. Set the closing date and property tax. The tool prorates tax by days.
  5. Optionally turn on capital gains. Enter your purchase price and improvements.
  6. Open the other tabs. Compare commission rates and find the price for your target.

The net proceeds formula

Net proceeds = sale price − commission − concessions − transfer tax − title and escrow − attorney − repairs and staging − warranty − other costs ± property tax proration − mortgage and lien payoff.

Worked example. Sell for 450,000 with a 5% commission (22,500), 0.5% transfer tax (2,250), 1% title and escrow (4,500), 5,000 of repairs, staging and warranty, about 5,400 of property tax proration (a mid-year closing in a state where tax is paid in arrears) and a 210,000 payoff. Costs total about 39,700 and you net about 200,300, which is 44.5% of the price. The mortgage payoff is the largest deduction, but the selling costs are the part you can influence.

Capital gains on a home sale (United States)

In the United States a seller can exclude up to 250,000 of gain, or 500,000 for married couples filing jointly, if the home was their main home for at least two of the last five years. Gain above the exclusion is generally taxed at long-term capital gains rates of 0%, 15% or 20% depending on income. Depreciation claimed on any rental or business use is taxed separately, up to 25%, and does not qualify for the exclusion. State taxes may also apply.

This tool shows a simplified federal illustration so you can see if tax is likely to matter. It is not a tax return. Ask a tax professional about your own facts, including the net investment income tax, partial exclusions and timing.

Using net proceeds to compare offers

Two offers with the same price can leave you with very different cash. Concessions, repair credits, a buyer who wants you to pay more closing costs, a different closing date or a lower commission can each change the result. Enter each offer's terms and compare the net proceeds and the price needed to hit your target, rather than the headline number.

Ways sellers raise their net

  • Negotiate commission, and compare agents on service as well as rate.
  • Do repairs that return more than they cost and skip large renovations that rarely pay back.
  • Check for a lower-cost closing or title provider where you are allowed to choose.
  • Pay down or refinance nothing in the final weeks, to avoid payoff surprises.
  • Time the closing to reduce overlap of two housing payments.

Tips for a more accurate estimate

  • Ask your lender for a written payoff letter with a per diem.
  • Ask your agent for a local net sheet to compare.
  • Include the full cost of repairs the buyer will request after inspection.
  • Run the numbers at three prices, including one below your hope.
  • Keep a cash buffer for moving, overlap and deposits on the next home.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the sale price as the money you will receive.
  • Forgetting the second mortgage, HELOC or other liens.
  • Leaving out property tax and HOA proration.
  • Ignoring repair credits that buyers request.
  • Assuming no capital gains tax without checking the exclusion rules.

A worked example with and without tax

Suppose you sell for 450,000 with a 210,000 mortgage, a 5% commission and the default costs. The estimate shows roughly 200,000 of net proceeds. If you bought for 250,000, spent 30,000 on improvements and the home was your main residence, the gain after selling costs is about 140,000, well inside the 250,000 single-filer exclusion, so no federal capital gains tax is due. Change the original purchase price to 100,000 and the gain rises to about 320,000, which leaves roughly 70,000 taxable after the exclusion. At a 15% rate that is about 10,600 of tax. If the home was not your main residence the exclusion disappears and the tax rises sharply. This is why checking the basis and use history before you list is worth the time.

Commission, discounts and what you get for the fee

Commission is split between the listing and buyer agents and is negotiable. Discount and flat-fee models exist, but a lower fee may come with fewer services, and some buyers' agents may expect the usual buyer-side share. Instead of comparing only rates, compare what is included: pricing advice, photography, marketing, showings, negotiation and help with inspection requests. The commission table shows the dollar value of each half-point on your price, which makes the trade-off concrete. Check current industry rules where you live, because the way buyer agent compensation is agreed and disclosed has been changing in some countries.

Timing, overlap and the next purchase

Net proceeds are the cash you receive at closing, but the next purchase usually has its own timeline. Think about the overlap between selling and buying, any bridge loan or rent-back, the deposit you must pay before your sale closes, and moving costs. If you need a certain down payment for the next home, use the price-for-my-target tab to see what price you need, then compare it with comparable sales. If the number looks out of reach, consider whether you should wait, reduce costs or look at a lower-priced next home.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate net proceeds from selling a house?

Start with the sale price. Subtract agent commission, closing costs, transfer taxes, repairs and concessions, adjust for property tax proration, and subtract the payoff of your mortgage and any liens. What remains is your net proceeds.

What is a seller net sheet?

A seller net sheet is an estimate of the money you will receive at closing. Agents prepare one before you list. This calculator creates the same kind of estimate and lets you change the inputs yourself.

How much does it cost to sell a house?

Many sellers pay roughly 6% to 10% of the price once commission, closing, repairs and concessions are included. Your actual cost depends on location, commission, transfer taxes and the condition of the home.

What agent commission should I use?

Commission is negotiable and varies by market and agent. Use the total your listing agreement says, and test lower and higher figures in the commission scenarios table.

What closing costs do sellers pay?

Common seller costs include title insurance, escrow or settlement fees, attorney fees, transfer or recording taxes, and prorated property tax and HOA dues. Who pays what varies by area and contract.

What is property tax proration?

At closing, property tax is split between you and the buyer by days of ownership. If tax is paid in arrears you owe the buyer for the days you owned the home this year. If it is paid in advance the buyer credits you for the days left.

Do I pay capital gains tax when I sell my house?

In the United States you may exclude up to 250,000 of gain (500,000 if married filing jointly) if you owned and lived in the home as your main home for at least two of the last five years. Gains above that, or on other homes, can be taxed.

How is capital gains calculated on a home?

Gain = sale price − selling costs − cost basis. Basis is what you paid plus capital improvements. The optional tax section estimates this and applies the home-sale exclusion.

What is depreciation recapture?

If you claimed depreciation because you rented out the home or used part of it for business, that part of the gain can be taxed at up to 25% in the United States and does not qualify for the home-sale exclusion.

Can I find the price I need to net a specific amount?

Yes. The price for my target tab works backwards from the cash you want to receive and shows the sale price needed, before and optionally after tax.

How do seller concessions affect my proceeds?

Concessions reduce what you receive dollar for dollar. A 3% concession on a 450,000 sale costs 13,500, so compare offers by net proceeds rather than headline price.

Should I make repairs before selling?

Repairs that raise the price by more than they cost, or fix issues that would derail a sale, can be worth it. Others are better handled with a price reduction or credit. Use the calculator to compare the net in each case.

What if I have a second mortgage or HELOC?

Enter it in the second mortgage field. Every lien must be paid from the sale proceeds at closing, so it reduces your cash dollar for dollar.

How accurate is this net proceeds estimate?

It is as accurate as your inputs. Ask your agent or title company for a payoff statement and fee estimate and enter those figures. The final number is on your settlement statement.

Does it include moving costs?

You can add them in other fixed costs if you want your net to reflect them, although they are not paid at closing.

Is this calculator for the United States only?

The sale math works anywhere. The property tax proration and the optional capital gains section follow United States rules. Leave capital gains off if you are elsewhere.

Can I print or export the result?

Is my data private?

Yes. All calculations run in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server or stored.
