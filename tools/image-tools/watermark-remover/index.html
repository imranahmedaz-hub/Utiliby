How reverse alpha blending works

When a semi-transparent watermark (like the Gemini sparkle, a studio logo, or a text overlay) is placed over an image, each pixel is blended mathematically:

Blended = (1 − α) × Original + α × Watermark

If you know the watermark colour (typically white) and can estimate the transparency value α, you can solve for the original pixel value exactly:

Original = (Blended − α × Watermark) / (1 − α)

This calculation is applied independently to the red, green and blue channels of every pixel inside the masked region. Unlike AI inpainting, which hallucinates content, reverse alpha blending restores the actual underlying pixels with zero blur, zero smearing and no loss of texture or grain.

The challenge is estimating α accurately. This tool provides an alpha slider so you can tune it until the watermark disappears. For most semi-transparent white watermarks, α between 0.3 and 0.6 works well.

How inpainting works

For opaque watermarks — solid logos, black text, date stamps — reverse alpha blending cannot help because there is no original information to recover. Instead, the tool uses inpainting: it fills the masked region with plausible content derived from the surrounding pixels.

This tool uses a fast multi-pass diffusion algorithm. On each pass, every masked pixel that has at least one non-masked neighbour is updated to the distance-weighted average of its known neighbours. Pixels fill inward from the boundary of the mask, propagating colour and texture from the outside in. After several passes, the entire masked region is filled with content that blends naturally with the surrounding image.

Inpainting works best when the masked area is thin (a watermark stroke), sits on a relatively uniform background (sky, wall, gradient, simple texture), or is small relative to the image. For large masks on highly detailed backgrounds, artifacts may be visible — this is a limitation of any diffusion-based inpainting method and is not specific to this tool.

Tips for the best results

  • Mask only the watermark. Extra mask area removes extra pixels unnecessarily. Be precise.
  • Add a small margin. Watermarks often have a subtle edge or shadow. Mask slightly beyond the visible boundary.
  • Try "Auto" method first. It compares alpha and inpaint results and picks the better one.
  • Tune the alpha slider. For semi-transparent white watermarks, 0.35–0.55 usually works best. If the result looks too dark, lower alpha. If the watermark is still faintly visible, raise it.
  • Use the compare slider. Zoom into the boundary between original and fixed to check for artifacts before downloading.
  • Work at full resolution. Don't downscale the image before processing — detail matters.
