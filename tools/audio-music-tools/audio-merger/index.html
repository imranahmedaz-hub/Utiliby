What is a free audio merger online?

A free audio merger online is a web tool that joins several audio recordings into one continuous file. You might use it to combine podcast segments, stitch together voice memos, build a mixtape from separate songs, join the chapters of an audiobook, or chain sound effects for a video. Instead of opening a full digital audio workstation, you add your files, put them in order, and download a single merged MP3 or WAV.

This audio merger goes further than a simple "join" button. You can drag clips into order, trim each one on its waveform, choose between seamless, silence-gap, crossfade and overlay joins, match the loudness of clips recorded at different volumes, shorten silences, add fades, and export lossless WAV or compact MP3. A live timeline shows exactly where every clip lands before you commit, and a timestamped tracklist is generated automatically for podcast and video descriptions.

Everything happens inside your browser. Your audio is decoded and mixed on your own device and is not uploaded, which makes it a sensible choice for confidential interviews, unreleased music and personal recordings.

How to merge audio files online

  1. Add your files. Click the upload area or drag and drop MP3, WAV, OGG, M4A, AAC or FLAC files. You can add up to 50 files, and you can mix formats.
  2. Set the order. Drag a clip by its grip, or use the up and down buttons. The Sort menu orders clips by name (numbers are sorted naturally, so track 2 comes before track 10) or by length.
  3. Trim each clip. Drag the round handles on a waveform or type exact times. Click the waveform to listen from that point, or press Play on a clip to hear its trimmed section.
  4. Choose a join mode. Pick Seamless, Silence gap, Crossfade or Overlay. The timeline above the clip list updates instantly so you can see the effect.
  5. Tune the sound. Optionally match loudness, remove silence, add a global fade in or out, or set a clip gain in decibels.
  6. Choose the output. Select WAV (16-bit, 24-bit or 32-bit float) or MP3 and a bitrate. Open More options for sample rate, channels and file name.
  7. Merge and preview. Click Merge audio, watch the progress, then listen to the preview and its waveform before you download.
  8. Export extras. Copy the timestamped tracklist, download a CUE sheet or CSV, or save the project settings for later.

How the audio merger works

Merging audio is not the same as gluing two files together byte by byte. MP3, AAC and OGG are compressed streams, and even WAV files can differ in sample rate, bit depth and channel count. To join them correctly the tool follows a fixed pipeline, and every step runs locally.

  1. Decode. Your browser converts each file into raw samples using the Web Audio API. It decodes at its own native rate, usually 44.1 or 48 kHz.
  2. Plan. The tool reads your trim points, silence settings and join mode and builds a timeline in seconds, which is what you see in the live preview.
  3. Mix. Clips are placed on the timeline with sample-accurate positions. Crossfades use equal-power or linear curves. Mono and surround sources are converted to the output channel layout using standard mixing weights.
  4. Finish. Optional loudness matching, peak normalisation, a clipping guard and raised-cosine master fades are applied, and tiny 3 millisecond fades at the joins remove audible clicks.
  5. Encode. The result is written as WAV, with optional dither for 16-bit output, or encoded to MP3.

Seamless, silence gap, crossfade or overlay?

The join mode controls how neighbouring clips meet. Choosing well makes a bigger difference to the result than any other setting.

Seamless join

Clips are placed back to back with nothing between them. Use it for audiobook chapters, gapless albums and any recording that was split into parts and should sound like one take. Keep the click-removal option on unless you need a bit-exact join.

Silence gap

A pause is inserted between clips. For speech, a gap of 0.3 to 1 second sounds more natural than a hard cut, and it lets listeners process the change of topic or speaker. Use longer gaps for chapter breaks.

Crossfade

The end of one clip fades out while the next fades in over the same stretch of time. It hides small differences in tempo and loudness and sounds professional on music. The crossfade is automatically limited to half the shorter clip, so short clips are not consumed.

Overlay

Every clip starts at 0:00 and the audio is summed. Use it to layer narration over music, add a sound bed or combine separate stems. Watch the level: overlapping clips add together, so enable Prevent clipping or lower a clip with its gain control.

Supported audio formats

The tool accepts whatever your browser can decode. In practice that covers the formats below, and you can combine them freely in one project.

  • MP3 — the most widely used compressed audio format.
  • WAV — uncompressed PCM audio, common in recording and editing.
  • OGG Vorbis and Opus — open compressed formats used on the web and in games.
  • M4A and AAC — the format behind many phone voice memos and music downloads.
  • FLAC — lossless compressed audio, supported in current browsers.
  • WebM audio — typical for recordings made in a browser.

Codec support depends on your browser and operating system. If one file will not decode, convert it to WAV or MP3 with another program and add it again. Audio inside a video file (MP4 or WebM) is often decodable too, which is a quick way to extract and join soundtracks.

Loudness matching explained

Recordings made in different places rarely have the same volume. If you join them without correction, listeners keep reaching for the volume control. The Match clip loudness option measures each clip's average level with a gated RMS method, which ignores near-silent passages, and applies a gain so the clips sit near their common average. The correction is limited to 20 dB either way, and a boost is never allowed to push a clip beyond a safe peak.

Peak normalise is different: it scales the whole finished mix so its highest peak reaches −1 dBFS. The two work well together, and the Match + peak normalise option runs both in that order.

Note: RMS matching is a practical approximation, not a certified loudness meter. Platforms that measure integrated loudness in LUFS (as described in ITU-R BS.1770 and EBU R128) may still adjust the final file. For broadcast delivery, check the result with a dedicated loudness meter.

Keeping the best audio quality

  • Merge to WAV first. Create the master as 24-bit or 32-bit float WAV, then encode MP3 once if you need it.
  • Avoid repeated lossy conversions. Each MP3 or AAC encode discards some information.
  • Mind the sample rate. Auto uses your browser's native rate, usually 44.1 or 48 kHz. Pick 44.1 kHz for music destined for CD-style players and 48 kHz for video.
  • Leave headroom. Peak normalise to −1 dBFS or use the clipping guard so MP3 encoding does not overshoot.
  • Use dither for 16-bit. Low-level noise added before reducing bit depth avoids harsh quantisation distortion on quiet fades.
  • Do not upsample for quality. Exporting a 22 kHz recording at 48 kHz makes a bigger file, not a better one.

Tips for a clean merge

  • Trim first. Cut false starts, clicks and long silences on each waveform before you merge.
  • Use crossfade for music and gaps for speech. A hard cut is only right when the clips are meant to be continuous.
  • Name files with numbers. A prefix such as 01, 02, 03 plus the natural sort gives the right order in one click.
  • Preview before you share. Scrub through the merged waveform and listen to every join at least once.
  • Save the project. If you may need to re-do the merge, save the project file; reloading it restores order, trims and gains.
  • Keep the originals. This tool never alters your source files, but keep backups anyway.

Online tools vs desktop software

Desktop programs such as Audacity, command-line tools such as FFmpeg and SoX, and full audio workstations can do everything a merger does and more, including multitrack editing, noise reduction, compression and batch scripting. They are the right choice for complex projects, very large files, or precise mastering.

A browser-based merger is the faster choice when you only need to join files: there is nothing to install, it works on any computer, and it can run on a locked-down work laptop. Many online mergers upload your audio to a server, apply file-size caps or add a watermark. This tool avoids those trade-offs by processing locally, but it is limited by your browser's memory and codec support. If a job outgrows it, use one of the desktop options above.

Privacy: why browser-based matters

Audio often contains private information: voices, names, business discussions or unreleased work. Server-based converters must receive your file to process it, which creates questions about retention, access and jurisdiction. This tool decodes and mixes audio in your own browser tab, so your recordings are not transmitted to Utiliby. The optional MP3 encoder is a public script downloaded once; your audio is not sent when it loads.

The Remember settings option stores only the tool settings in your browser and is off by default. Project files contain settings and trim points, never audio. Clearing your browser data removes anything stored locally.

Frequently asked questions

What is a free audio merger online?

A free audio merger online is a web tool that joins several audio files into one continuous track without installing software. This one runs entirely in your browser: it decodes your files, mixes them on a timeline and exports a single WAV or MP3 file, and your audio is never uploaded to a server.

How do I merge audio files online for free?

Click the upload area or drop two or more audio files onto it. Drag the clips into the order you want, trim each one on its waveform, choose a join mode (seamless, silence gap, crossfade or overlay), then click Merge audio. When it finishes, preview the result and download it as WAV or MP3.

How do I merge MP3 files into one file?

Add your MP3 files, put them in order, pick Seamless or Crossfade, set the output format to MP3 and click Merge audio. The tool decodes every MP3, joins the audio and encodes a single new MP3. Because MP3 is a lossy format, the output is re-encoded once; choose 192 kbps or higher for music, or WAV if you need a lossless master.

Does this tool upload my audio files?

No. Decoding, mixing, loudness analysis and encoding all run locally in your browser tab using the Web Audio API and JavaScript. Your files are not sent to Utiliby or any other server. The only network request the tool can make is a one-time download of the open-source MP3 encoder script, and only if you choose MP3 output.

Which audio formats can I merge?

You can merge any format your browser can decode, which normally includes MP3, WAV, OGG Vorbis, Opus, M4A and AAC, FLAC and WebM audio. Support differs slightly between browsers and operating systems, and a file the browser cannot decode is flagged and ignored. You can mix different formats in the same merge.

Can I join WAV and MP3 files together?

Yes. Every input is decoded to raw audio first, so the original container does not matter. WAV, MP3, M4A, OGG and FLAC clips can sit on the same timeline and are converted to a single output sample rate and channel layout before they are mixed.

What output formats are supported?

You can export 16-bit WAV, 24-bit WAV, 32-bit float WAV or MP3 at 64, 96, 128, 160, 192, 256 or 320 kbps. WAV is lossless and best for editing or archiving. MP3 is smaller and better for sharing, podcasts and phones.

What is the difference between seamless, silence gap, crossfade and overlay?

Seamless places clips back to back with no gap. Silence gap inserts a pause between clips, which sounds natural for speech. Crossfade overlaps the end of one clip with the start of the next while fading one out and the other in. Overlay lays every clip over the others from time zero so you can layer a voice over music.

What is a crossfade and how long should it be?

A crossfade blends the tail of one clip into the head of the next. Around 2 to 4 seconds suits most music, 5 to 8 seconds is common for DJ-style blends, and 0.1 to 0.5 seconds smooths a tight edit. The tool limits a crossfade to half of the shorter neighbouring clip so a short clip is never swallowed.

What is equal-power crossfade versus linear?

A linear crossfade fades the volume of the two clips by straight lines that add up to full level, which is right for identical or highly similar audio. An equal-power crossfade uses sine and cosine curves that keep the perceived loudness steady for different songs. Equal-power is the default and the better choice for music.

How do I trim a clip before merging?

Drag the two round handles on the clip waveform, or type exact start and end times such as 0:12.50 into the boxes beneath it. Click anywhere on the waveform to listen from that point. Trimming never changes your original file; it only chooses which part of it goes into the merged track.

Can I remove silence between and inside clips?

Yes. Choose Clip edges to cut leading and trailing silence from every clip, or Edges and long pauses to also shorten internal pauses that exceed the minimum length you set. You control the silence threshold in decibels, so quiet speech is not mistaken for silence.

What does the loudness match option do?

Loudness match measures the average level of each clip and applies a gain so the clips sound similarly loud, within plus or minus 20 dB and without pushing any clip past a safe peak. It uses a gated RMS measurement, which is a practical approximation and not a certified LUFS meter. You can also nudge any clip by up to 30 dB manually.

What does peak normalise do?

Peak normalise scales the finished mix so its loudest sample sits at minus 1 dBFS. That removes clipping risk and makes use of the available headroom. It changes overall level only; it does not compress or limit the dynamics.

Why does the output sound clipped or distorted?

Clipping happens when the sum of the audio exceeds full scale, which is common with overlay mode or very loud sources. Turn on Prevent clipping, use peak normalise, lower individual clip gain, or export 32-bit float WAV, which cannot clip internally. Distortion can also be present in the source files themselves.

Why is my merged WAV file so large?

WAV stores uncompressed audio. A stereo 16-bit file at 44.1 kHz uses about 10.6 MB per minute, and a 24-bit file at 48 kHz uses about 17.3 MB per minute. Export MP3 at 128 to 192 kbps for a file roughly ten times smaller, or choose mono when the content is speech.

Is MP3 export lossless?

No. MP3 is a lossy format, so exporting MP3 re-encodes the audio. If you merge MP3 files into an MP3, the audio is decoded and encoded again, which can add a small amount of loss. For the highest quality, merge to WAV, and encode to MP3 only once at the end of your workflow.

Does the tool work offline?

After the page has loaded, merging to WAV works without an internet connection. MP3 export needs a one-time download of the open-source lamejs encoder from a public CDN, so use WAV if you are offline or if the download is blocked on your network.

How many files and how large can they be?

You can add up to 50 files of up to 300 MB each. The practical limit is your device memory, because decoded audio is uncompressed: one hour of stereo audio is roughly 600 MB in memory. The tool shows a memory estimate and warns you before a job becomes risky.

Why do my files sound slightly different in pitch or length?

They should not. If a result sounds too fast or too slow, the source files probably have different sample rates and a player is misreading them. This tool converts every clip to one output sample rate so playback speed stays correct. Check the Output format line to confirm the rate.

Can I merge audio files on my phone?

Yes. The tool works in current mobile browsers on iOS and Android. Large merges can run out of memory on phones, so for long projects use fewer clips, mono output or MP3, and keep the screen on until the merge is done.
