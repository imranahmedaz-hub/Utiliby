How audio merging works

Merging audio is not as simple as concatenating file bytes. Each audio file is a compressed stream (MP3, AAC, OGG) or a container (WAV, FLAC). To combine them, the tool performs four steps:

  1. Decode: Every file is decoded into raw PCM samples — an array of amplitude values at a specific sample rate (usually 44.1 kHz).
  2. Align: Sample rates and channel counts are matched so mixed inputs still produce a clean result.
  3. Render: An OfflineAudioContext schedules every clip at the correct position on a single timeline, applies fades and crossfades, and renders the combined audio faster than real time.
  4. Encode: The rendered buffer is encoded to WAV — a lossless, uncompressed format that preserves every sample.

All four steps happen locally in your browser tab. No audio data is ever sent over the network.

Seamless, crossfade or silence gap?

Choosing the right join mode makes a large difference to the final result.

Seamless join

Clips are placed back-to-back with no gap and no overlap. This is the correct mode for chapter joins in audiobooks, gapless albums, or any content where the clips are meant to be truly continuous.

Crossfade

The end of one clip and the start of the next overlap, with each fading out or in over a configurable duration. Crossfade produces a smooth musical transition and hides small timing differences between takes. It is best for music mixes, DJ sets and anywhere a hard boundary would sound jarring.

Silence gap

A short pause is inserted between clips. For spoken-word content — podcast segments, interviews, lessons, narration — a 0.3 to 1 second pause sounds more natural than a hard cut. The gap duration is configurable from 0.1 to 5 seconds.

Why merging happens locally in your browser

Most free audio mergers upload your files to a remote server, process them there, and send back the result. That model has three problems:

  • Privacy. Your audio — which might be a confidential interview, an unreleased track or a personal voice memo — passes through a third-party server.
  • Retention. Many services keep uploaded files for hours or days. You have no visibility into what happens to them.
  • Limits. Server-based tools often impose file size caps, daily conversion limits or paid tiers.

This audio merger sidesteps all three. Everything happens in your browser using the Web Audio API. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and there is no limit on how many files you merge.

Supported audio formats

This tool accepts any audio format your browser can decode. In practice that means:

  • MP3 — the most widely used compressed format
  • WAV — uncompressed, lossless audio
  • OGG Vorbis — open-source compressed format
  • M4A and AAC — Apple and MPEG-4 audio
  • FLAC — lossless compressed audio (modern browsers)
  • WebM audio — commonly used in browser recordings

The output is always WAV, which is lossless, universally compatible, and preserves every sample without re-encoding artifacts. You can convert WAV to MP3 afterwards with any audio converter if you need a smaller file.

Tips for the best merge results

  • Trim before merging. Removing silence and false starts at the head and tail of each clip produces a tighter result.
  • Match bitrates where possible. Mixed inputs are normalized automatically, but starting from similar formats produces cleaner output.
  • Use crossfade for music, silence gap for speech. Hard cuts work best only when the clips are intended to be truly continuous.
  • Enable normalization for mixed sources. If clips were recorded at different volumes, peak normalization evens them out.
  • Preview before downloading. Listen to the merged result in the built-in player to catch order or gap mistakes.
