What this free MP4 to MP3 converter does

An MP4 to MP3 converter takes the soundtrack out of a video and saves it as a stand-alone audio file. People do this to listen to a lecture on a commute, keep the audio of a recorded meeting, turn a webinar into a podcast episode, or pull a song from a video they filmed themselves. Most online converters ask you to upload the whole video to a server, wait in a queue, and accept a file size limit unless you pay. This free online tool works differently: your browser does the conversion on your own device, so the video never leaves your computer or phone.

You can drop several files at once, choose MP3 or lossless WAV, pick a bitrate, switch to mono for speech, lower the sample rate to shrink files, trim a section by start and end time, and normalize the volume. It accepts MP4, MOV, M4V, WebM, MKV and many plain audio formats, as long as your browser can decode them. There is no watermark, no account and no daily quota.

Choosing the right bitrate

Bitrate is the amount of data used for each second of sound. Higher numbers mean larger files and, up to a point, better quality. The table gives practical starting points.

A video soundtrack is usually AAC at 128 to 256 kbps already. Choosing 320 kbps will not bring back detail that was removed when the video was made, so there is rarely a reason to pick more than the source used. When in doubt, 192 kbps is a safe default.

Will converting MP4 to MP3 lose quality?

Yes, a little, and it is worth understanding why. MP3 and AAC are lossy formats: they save space by discarding sounds the ear is least likely to notice. When you convert one lossy format to another, the sound is decoded to raw audio and then compressed again, and the second pass can only discard more. At 192 kbps and above, most listeners cannot hear the difference. At low bitrates the extra loss can show up as a watery or swishy sound on cymbals and applause.

If you need the best possible copy, choose WAV, which stores the decoded audio with no further compression. If you only need the original audio track without any re-encoding, a tool that copies the stream into an M4A file is more suitable, but it produces M4A rather than MP3, which some older players cannot open.

Supported video and audio formats

The converter does not have its own video decoder. It asks your browser to decode the file, which means anything your browser can play will usually work. Chrome, Edge, Safari and Firefox all handle MP4 with AAC or MP3 audio, WebM with Opus or Vorbis, WAV, OGG and FLAC. MOV from phones is normally fine. MKV works when it contains common codecs. Audio in AC-3, DTS or very old codecs may not decode in some browsers.

If a file refuses to load, open it in your browser first: if it plays with sound, it should convert. If it does not, try another browser, because codec support differs between them. Safari tends to be strongest with Apple formats, and Chrome and Firefox are strongest with WebM and Ogg.

Trim, mono, sample rate and normalize explained

  • Start and end: keep only the part you need. Typing 2:10 as the start and 5:00 as the end converts exactly that range. This is handy for pulling a song from a long video or skipping a long intro.
  • Mono: mixes both channels into one. It halves the file size and is ideal for speech. Do not use it for music with a wide stereo image.
  • Sample rate: the number of samples per second. 44100 Hz and 48000 Hz are standard. 22050 Hz is enough for voice, and 16000 Hz is common for speech to text tools.
  • Normalize: scales the file so that its highest peak is -1 dB. It makes a quiet recording louder without clipping, but background noise gets louder too.

Large files and long recordings

Because everything runs on your device, the limit is memory rather than a server cap. The tool decodes the audio to uncompressed samples before encoding. A mono hour at 44.1 kHz takes around 650 MB as 32-bit samples, and stereo takes about twice that. Computers with 8 GB or more handle one-hour files comfortably. Phones and older laptops may struggle past 20 to 30 minutes.

To work with long recordings, choose Mono, lower the sample rate, or convert in sections with the start and end fields. Closing other tabs also frees memory. If the browser reports that the tab ran out of memory, reload the page and try a shorter section.

Troubleshooting

  • No sound in the result: check that the video has an audio track and plays with sound in your browser.
  • The file will not load: the codec may be unsupported. Try another browser or re-export the video as MP4 with AAC audio.
  • The page freezes or reloads: the file is too large for available memory. Use Mono, a lower sample rate or a shorter section.
  • MP3 option fails to start: the encoder could not be downloaded. Check your connection, or choose WAV.
  • Output is too quiet: turn on Normalize.
  • Output sounds thin: you may have chosen Mono or a low sample rate for music. Switch back to stereo and 44100 Hz.

How this compares with desktop tools

Desktop programs such as VLC, Audacity and FFmpeg are powerful, and FFmpeg can copy the audio stream without re-encoding. They require installing software, and for some, learning commands. Online converters that upload your video are convenient but raise privacy questions for anything sensitive, and they often cap file size. A browser-based converter sits in between: nothing to install, no upload, and enough options for most everyday jobs. It is not a replacement for a full editor when you need noise reduction, multi-track editing or exact stream copying.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert MP4 to MP3 for free?

Drop your MP4 on the box above, choose MP3 and a bitrate, and click Convert to audio. The file is processed in your browser, so there is no sign-up, no watermark and no upload. When it finishes, the MP3 downloads automatically for a single file, or you can download each result or a ZIP for several files.

Is my video uploaded to a server?

No. The video is read and decoded on your own device. The page downloads an open-source MP3 encoder from a public CDN the first time you choose MP3, but your video and audio are never sent anywhere. WAV output needs no download at all.

Which video formats can it convert?

Anything your browser can play, which usually includes MP4, MOV, M4V, WebM, MKV with common codecs, 3GP and many audio formats such as M4A, AAC, OGG, FLAC and WAV. Support comes from your browser, so a rare codec such as DTS audio may not decode. If a file fails, try another browser or re-export the audio from the source.

What MP3 bitrate should I choose?

Use 192 kbps for a good all-round result, 256 or 320 kbps for music you care about, 128 kbps for everyday listening and 64 to 96 kbps mono for speech such as lectures and meetings. Higher bitrates make larger files without improving a source that was already compressed.

Will the sound quality get worse?

Video files already store compressed audio, usually AAC. Converting to MP3 compresses it a second time, so a small quality loss is unavoidable. At 192 kbps or higher the difference is hard to hear. If you need no loss at all, choose WAV, which is uncompressed but much larger.

Is there a file size limit?

We set no limit. The practical limit is your device memory, because the audio is decoded to raw samples. As a rule, one hour of stereo audio needs roughly 1.3 GB of free memory while converting. For longer recordings use Mono, a lower sample rate, or the trim fields to convert in parts.

Can I convert several videos at once?

Yes. Add as many files as you like, click Convert, then download each MP3 or all of them as a ZIP. Files are processed one after another to keep memory use low.

Can I convert only part of a video?

Yes. Enter a start time and an end time, for example 1:30 and 4:45, and only that section is converted. Leave both blank for the whole file. Times accept seconds, m:ss or h:mm:ss.

What does Normalize do?

It raises or lowers the whole file so the loudest peak sits at -1 dB. It does not change the balance between loud and quiet parts. It is useful for a recording that came out too quiet, but it will not fix background noise.

Should I pick mono or stereo?

Keep stereo for music. Choose mono for speech, phone calls and screen recordings, because mono files are about half the size and sound the same when there is only one voice.

Why did my file fail to convert?

The most common reasons are a video with no audio track, an audio codec your browser cannot decode, a damaged file, or not enough memory for a very long recording. Try a shorter section, a different browser, or play the file once to confirm it has sound.

Does it work on iPhone and Android?

Yes, in a current browser. Pick a video from your photos or files, convert, and save the MP3. Very large videos can run out of memory on phones, so trim long clips first.

Can I extract audio from a YouTube link?

No. This tool only works with files that you already have on your device. It does not download videos from websites, and you should respect the terms of any service and the rights of the creator.

Is the MP3 the same length as the video?

Yes, unless you trim it. Silence at the start or end of the video audio is kept. Very small differences of a few milliseconds can occur because of how MP3 frames are built.

What is the difference between MP3 and WAV here?

MP3 is compressed and small and plays everywhere. WAV is uncompressed 16-bit audio, about ten times larger, and is a better choice if you will edit the audio further.

Does it keep the title, artist and cover art?

No. The output is a plain audio file without tags or artwork. You can add tags afterwards in a music player or tag editor.

What can I print or save from the page?

Print / save summary prints only the settings and the list of converted files, and Copy summary copies the same list as text. The audio files are the main download.
