What a dice probability calculator does

Dice feel random, but the odds are exact. A dice probability calculator turns a roll such as 3d6, d20 with advantage or 4d6 drop lowest into the precise chance of each result, so you can decide whether to attack, pick a build or design an encounter with real numbers instead of hunches.

This free online tool computes the full probability distribution for sums of dice with a modifier, keep-highest and keep-lowest rolls, advantage, disadvantage and best-of-three rolls. It also handles success-counting dice pools and a roll-versus-roll comparison. You get the chance of hitting a target, the odds as "1 in N", the average, standard deviation, median, the most likely result and a chart. Everything runs in your browser.

How to use this calculator

  1. Choose a tab. Dice roll for one roll, Dice pool for success counting, Compare rolls for contests.
  2. Enter the dice. Number of dice, sides per die and a modifier. Use a preset such as d20 advantage or 4d6 drop lowest to start.
  3. Pick how the roll works. Add the dice, keep the highest or lowest, advantage, disadvantage or best of three.
  4. Set your target. Choose at least, more than, at most, less than or exactly, then enter the number.
  5. Read the chance. The green bars in the chart show which results meet your target. Open the full table for every result.

Ability scores: 3d6, 4d6 drop lowest and more

Rolling for ability scores is a classic use of keep-highest rolls. With 3d6 the average is 10.5 and an 18 appears 1 time in 216. With 4d6 drop lowest the average climbs to about 12.24 and the chance of at least 15 for a single score is about 23.1%, while at least 18 is about 1.6%. This is why 4d6 drop lowest feels generous: it removes most bad rolls.

Use the keep-highest option to test your own variants, such as 5d6 keep the highest three, or 2d20 keep the highest one for a quick comparison with advantage.

Dice pools and success counting

In many systems you roll a handful of dice and count how many beat a threshold. The chance of one success is (sides − target + 1) ÷ sides, and the number of successes follows a binomial distribution. Six six-sided dice with success on 5 or 6 give each die a 1 in 3 chance, an expected 2.0 successes and about a 64.9% chance of at least two. Use the pool tab to find how adding one more die changes the odds of reaching the number of successes you need.

Comparing rolls and contests

Opposed rolls and contested checks compare two totals. The compare tab shows the exact chance that A wins, ties or loses. A flat bonus matters less than it seems when the dice are wide: a +3 on 2d6 against a d20 is a coin flip, with A winning about 45% and B about 50%, and ties of about 5%. If ties favour the attacker, add the tie chance to the win chance.

Tips for using dice odds

  • Think in terms of the chance of the result you need, not the average.
  • Remember that probabilities describe the long run. A 90% roll still fails one time in ten.
  • Use the standard deviation to see how swingy a roll is: fewer, larger dice are swingier.
  • For game design, compare two options by chance of success and average result together.
  • Check the rules of your game for rerolls and special cases that change the maths.

Common dice probability mistakes

  • Gambler's fallacy. Past rolls do not change future ones for fair dice.
  • Adding probabilities of independent rolls. Two d6 do not have a 2 in 6 chance of a six; it is 11 in 36, about 30.6%.
  • Assuming averages equal likely. The average of 2d6 is 7, but 7 only occurs 1 time in 6.
  • Mixing up "at least" and "more than". A difference of one on a d20 is 5 percentage points.

Dice in game design: d12 or 2d6?

Two dice rolls can have a similar average and feel completely different. A d12 averages 6.5 and a 2d6 averages 7, but the d12 has a standard deviation of about 3.45 while 2d6 has about 2.42. The d12 is flat and swingy, with every value from 1 to 12 equally likely. The 2d6 clusters around 7, with 6, 7 and 8 together making up 44% of rolls. Designers choose flat distributions for excitement and bell curves for reliability. Use the compare tab and the distribution chart to see the difference for any pair.

Modifiers shift the whole distribution but do not change its shape. On a d20 each +1 modifier is worth 5 percentage points of chance until you hit the 5% minimum or 95% maximum. Advantage at a 50% base chance is worth about +5; at 30% it is worth about +4.2; and it is worth less at both extremes.

Critical hits and natural 20s

A natural 20 has a 5% chance on one d20. Over two attacks in a turn the chance of at least one is 1 − 0.95² = 9.75%, the same as one attack with advantage. Over three attacks it is 14.3%. Over ten attacks in a fight it is about 40%. These numbers explain why extra attacks matter so much to damage output: the more rolls you make, the more often the rare high result shows up. Use the keep-highest option or the dice pool tab to explore similar questions for your own game.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate dice probability?

Count the ways to get the result and divide by the total number of equally likely outcomes. For two six-sided dice there are 36 outcomes, and 6 of them sum to 7, so the chance of a 7 is 6/36 = 16.7%. This calculator does the counting exactly for you.

What is the probability of rolling a certain number or higher?

Add the probabilities of every result at or above the number. Choose "at least" in the dice roll tab and enter your target, and the tool shows the chance and the odds as 1 in N.

How does advantage work in probability terms?

You roll twice and keep the higher result. The chance of getting at least a target T on a d20 becomes 1 − (chance of missing)². At least 11 goes from 50% to 75%. At least 15 goes from 30% to 51%.

What is disadvantage in D&D?

You roll twice and keep the lower result, so both rolls must be high. At least 11 drops from 50% to 25%, and at least 15 drops from 30% to 9%.

How much does advantage increase the average roll on a d20?

A normal d20 averages 10.5. With advantage it averages 13.825, an increase of about 3.3 points. With disadvantage it averages 7.175.

What is 4d6 drop lowest?

It is a common method for generating ability scores: roll four six-sided dice and add the highest three. The average is about 12.24, compared with 10.5 for 3d6. Use the keep-highest option with 4 dice and keep 3.

What is the chance of rolling an 18 with 4d6 drop lowest?

About 1.62%. With plain 3d6 it is 0.46%, which is 1 in 216.

What does keep highest or keep lowest mean?

You roll a number of dice but only add some of them. 2d20 keep highest 1 is the same as advantage. The calculator supports keeping the top or bottom dice for up to 12 dice.

What is a dice pool?

A dice pool is a handful of dice where each die is a success or a failure, usually by meeting a target number. You count the successes. Many tabletop games use this, for example 6d6 with a success on 5 or 6.

How do I find the chance of at least 2 successes in a dice pool?

Use the dice pool tab. Enter the number of dice, sides, the success number and the successes needed. For 6d6 with success on 5+, the chance of at least 2 successes is about 64.9%.

How do I compare two different rolls?

Use the compare tab. It computes the exact chance that roll A beats, ties or loses to roll B. For example 2d6+3 against a d20 wins 45.0%, ties 5.0% and loses 50.0%.

What is standard deviation for dice?

It measures how spread out the results are. Rolling more dice makes results cluster around the average, so the standard deviation grows slower than the total.

Why do dice sums form a bell curve?

There are more ways to make middle totals than extremes. With 2d6 there is one way to roll 2 and six ways to roll 7. The more dice you add, the closer the shape gets to a normal bell curve.

Is a d100 the same as 2d10?

A d100 is uniform: each value from 1 to 100 has a 1% chance. Two d10s read as tens and units are also uniform. Adding two d10s is not the same, because the sum has a triangular shape.

Does this calculator handle exploding dice or rerolls?

No. It models fair dice with the options listed. Exploding dice and reroll rules vary by game and are not included.

Can I use it for board games and RPGs?

Yes, for any game that uses sums, keep-highest rolls, advantage or success counting. It is useful for Dungeons & Dragons style games, board games and war games.
