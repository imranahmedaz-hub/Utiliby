What this free online lesson plan template generator does

A lesson plan is the bridge between a curriculum and a classroom. A good one makes the next forty-five minutes predictable for the teacher, clear for the students and easy to hand over to a substitute or an observer. A poor one is either missing or so long that it is never used. This lesson plan template generator aims for the middle: complete enough to be professional and short enough to write quickly.

Start with the lesson details and objectives, choose a template, adjust the timing and fill in assessment and support. A timeline shows how the minutes are spent. A Bloom's taxonomy checker reads your objectives, flags vague verbs and shows the thinking level of each. Your work is saved automatically in your browser, and you can copy it, download a Word file or print only the plan.

How to write a lesson plan with this tool

  1. Fill in the details: title, subject, grade, date and lesson length.
  2. Write objectives, one per line, starting with an action verb. Read the checker's feedback and improve any weak verbs.
  3. Pick a template that matches your approach. It fills the sequence and fits it to your lesson length.
  4. Edit each phase: rename it, change the minutes, describe what you and the students will do, and move it up or down.
  5. Add assessment, differentiation and accommodations, then materials and vocabulary.
  6. Export: copy, download a Word file, or print the plan. After teaching, add a short reflection.

The parts of an effective lesson plan

  • Objectives. The destination. Everything else serves them.
  • Standards. The curriculum codes the lesson addresses.
  • Materials and vocabulary. What you must prepare and the words students must meet.
  • Sequence. Timed phases that move from engagement to practice to closure.
  • Assessment. How you will know who learned what, during and after the lesson.
  • Differentiation and accommodations. How every student reaches the goal.
  • Reflection. What worked, what to change next time.

Writing measurable learning objectives

The best objectives describe what a student can do at the end of the lesson, in words that you could see or hear. They answer three questions: what action, on what content, and to what standard. For example, Solve two-step equations with integer coefficients, getting at least four of five correct.

Avoid verbs such as understand, know, learn, appreciate and be aware of. They describe a hidden state of mind, so you cannot easily check them. The checker warns when an objective begins with one of these, and suggests observable alternatives such as explain, solve or compare.

Bloom's taxonomy and thinking levels

Bloom's taxonomy, as revised by Anderson and Krathwohl, orders cognitive skills from Remember through Understand, Apply, Analyse and Evaluate to Create. A balanced unit usually includes both foundations and higher-order tasks. The chart shows your lesson's spread, so if every objective sits at Remember you can see it at a glance and add an analysis or creation task. It is a guide, not a rule: a lesson that introduces vocabulary will rightly be weighted toward the lower levels.

Planning lesson timing

Teachers tend to overplan. A lesson with six activities usually delivers four. Leave a margin for transitions, questions and the unexpected, and plan a closure that can shrink without losing the main point. The tool shows planned minutes against lesson length, flags a plan that runs over and offers to scale every phase to fit. As a rough guide, aim for students to be actively practising for at least a third of the lesson.

Differentiation and inclusion

Differentiation does not mean three separate lessons. It means planning a few well-chosen adjustments in advance: scaffolds such as sentence starters or worked examples, choice in how students show learning, and extension tasks that deepen rather than simply add more. Note accommodations for individual students in the dedicated box, using the language of your school's support plans, and keep personal details out of any plan you share widely.

Accuracy, limits and responsible use

The generator organises your plan but does not write the pedagogy for you. Templates are general models, and the Bloom's checker uses a verb list that cannot judge the full meaning of a sentence. Always review the plan against your curriculum and school requirements.

Frequently asked questions

What is a lesson plan?

A teacher's written plan for one lesson. It states what students will learn, the activities and timing, the resources needed and how learning will be checked.

What should a lesson plan include?

A title and details, measurable learning objectives, standards, materials, a timed sequence of activities, assessment, differentiation, accommodations and a place for reflection.

Which lesson plan template should I choose?

The standard template suits most lessons. Use 5E for science and inquiry, gradual release for skills that need modelling, and the Hunter seven-step for direct instruction. You can edit any phase.

What is the 5E lesson plan model?

A sequence of Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate and Evaluate. Students investigate before the teacher formalises the concept, which suits science and enquiry work.

What is gradual release of responsibility?

A model where the teacher moves from I do, to We do, to You do together, to You do alone, so responsibility for the task shifts steadily to the students.

How do I write a good learning objective?

Start with a specific, observable action verb, name the content and say how well. For example, Compare two fractions with like denominators is clearer than Understand fractions.

What does the Bloom's taxonomy checker do?

It reads the first verb of each objective and shows its level from Remember to Create. It also warns about vague verbs such as understand or know that cannot be observed.

How do I make the timings add up?

Set the lesson length and press Fit phases to lesson length. The tool scales every phase so the minutes match, and the timeline and stats show any gap.

Can I add, remove or reorder phases?

Yes. Add a phase, delete one, or move phases up and down. The timeline updates at once.

Can I export the plan to Word?

Yes. Download a Word-compatible file that opens in Word and Google Docs, copy the plan as text, or print it.

What does printing include?

Only the plan: details, objectives, sequence, timeline chart, assessment and support sections. The rest of the page is hidden.

Is my plan saved?

Yes, automatically in your own browser so you can return to it. Nothing is uploaded. Download a copy to keep it permanently.

What is differentiation in a lesson plan?

Planned adjustments so every student can reach the objective, such as extra support, scaffolds, or extension tasks for those ready for more challenge.

What are accommodations?

Changes to how a student learns or shows learning, such as extra time, text to speech or a quiet space, usually set out in an individual plan.

How long should each part of the lesson be?

It depends on age and content. Many teachers keep direct instruction to around ten to fifteen minutes for younger students, then move to practice.

Can I use it for university or training sessions?

Yes. Use the blank template and rename the phases for lectures, workshops or corporate training.

Does it include state or national standards?

It has a free text box for your standards codes. You choose the standards, so the plan fits your own curriculum.
