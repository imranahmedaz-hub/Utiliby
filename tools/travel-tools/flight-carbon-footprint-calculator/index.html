What this free flight carbon footprint calculator does

Flying is one of the most carbon-intensive things most of us do, yet the numbers are rarely clear. This free online flight carbon footprint calculator estimates the CO2 emissions of a flight between any two of 114 major airports, or for a distance you enter. Choose the cabin class, one way or return, and the number of passengers, and it shows kilograms of CO2 per passenger and in total, with an optional uplift for the other climate effects of flying.

Charts compare cabin classes and show how flying stacks up against car, coach and rail, and the equivalents section translates the result into driving distance, tree-years, a share of a 2-tonne yearly budget and an offset cost. A second tab totals up to six flights for a year of travel, and the assumptions tab lets you edit every emission factor. Everything runs in your browser.

How to use the flight carbon footprint calculator

  1. Choose the airports from the list, or type a distance in kilometres.
  2. Select the cabin class and whether it is a return trip.
  3. Enter the number of passengers.
  4. Decide whether to include non-CO2 effects, which roughly double the climate impact.
  5. Read the emissions and the comparison with ground travel.
  6. Use the second tab to add up a year of flights, and the third to edit the assumptions.

How the emissions are estimated

The calculation has four steps. First, the great-circle distance between the airports is increased by a routing uplift, 8 percent by default, because real flights are longer than the straight line. Second, a factor in kilograms of CO2 per passenger-kilometre is chosen by distance: 0.18 for flights under 500 km, 0.14 up to 3,700 km and 0.10 beyond, which reflects the heavy fuel use of take-off on short hops. Third, a class multiplier is applied: for long-haul flights 1.6 for premium economy, 2.9 for business and 4.0 for first. Finally, return trips are doubled and, if selected, a multiplier of 1.9 covers non-CO2 effects.

For London to New York, the distance is 5,541 km, which becomes 5,984 km with the uplift. At 0.10 kg per km the one-way economy emissions are 598 kg per passenger, and 1,197 kg for the return. With the multiplier of 1.9, that is 2,274 kg CO2e, which is about 114 percent of a 2-tonne yearly budget. In business class the return is about 3,471 kg CO2 and 6,594 kg CO2e.

Comparing flying with other transport

For journeys of a few hundred kilometres, a train or coach produces a small fraction of the emissions of a flight. A typical average car emits about 0.17 kg of CO2 per kilometre, which is shared between everyone in it, so a full car can be competitive with flying even over long distances. Rail in Europe is commonly around 0.03 to 0.04 kg per passenger-kilometre and coaches about 0.03. The comparison chart assumes the ground route is 20 percent longer than the straight line and shows the difference. For island and intercontinental trips there may be no alternative, and the best choice is often to fly less often and stay longer.

Ways to reduce the footprint of your travel

  • Choose trains for trips under about 800 km where they are practical.
  • Fly economy, which cuts emissions per passenger by more than half compared with business.
  • Take direct flights, since take-off and landing use a lot of fuel.
  • Fly less often and stay longer, replacing several short trips with one long one.
  • Use video calls in place of some business travel.
  • Offset what remains through high-quality projects, as a supplement and not a substitute.

Reading flight emission numbers responsibly

Two figures appear in this tool. CO2 is the carbon dioxide released by burning fuel, and CO2e adds an allowance for the other warming effects of aircraft, such as contrails and nitrogen oxides, which scientists estimate roughly double the climate impact, though the exact multiplier is debated. Whichever you use, say which one you mean. The per-passenger figure is also an allocation: the plane burns the same fuel whether it is full or half empty, so the number per seat depends on how many seats are sold and how the aircraft is configured.

Estimates from different calculators can differ by 20 percent or more because of aircraft type, route and the way fuel, cargo and seat space are allocated. That is why the factors are editable. Use the numbers to compare choices, such as a direct flight against one with a stopover, economy against business class, or flying against a train, rather than to claim an exact total. If you choose to offset, look for credible, verified projects and treat offsetting as a complement to flying less, not a licence to fly more.

Limits and privacy

No simple calculator can know the aircraft, the passenger load or the route on the day. The factors here are typical planning values, and the non-CO2 multiplier is an area of active scientific debate. Official methodologies, such as those of aviation authorities and national governments, give a range of results. This tool is useful for comparing options and raising awareness, not for formal reporting. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

Frequently asked questions

How is the carbon footprint of a flight calculated?

The tool measures the great-circle distance, adds a routing uplift, multiplies by an emission factor per passenger-kilometre for the flight distance and a cabin class multiplier, then doubles it for a return trip. An optional multiplier covers non-CO2 effects.

How much CO2 does a flight from London to New York produce?

With the default factors, about 598 kg of CO2 per passenger one way in economy and 1,197 kg return. With the non-CO2 multiplier of 1.9, the return trip is about 2,274 kg CO2e.

What are non-CO2 effects?

Aircraft also affect the climate through contrails, cirrus clouds, nitrogen oxides and water vapour at altitude. Scientists estimate the total warming effect is roughly twice that of CO2 alone, though the figure is uncertain. You can switch the multiplier off or change it.

Why is business class worse than economy?

A business or first-class seat takes up much more floor area, so a plane carries fewer passengers, and each one is allocated a bigger share of the fuel burn. Multipliers of about 1.5 to 4 are commonly used.

Why are short flights worse per kilometre?

Take-off and climb use a large share of fuel, and that is spread over fewer kilometres on a short flight. The tool uses a higher factor for flights under 500 km.

What is the routing uplift?

Flights rarely follow the great-circle line because of airways, weather and air traffic control. A typical uplift of about 8 percent is added to the distance.

How accurate is this estimate?

It is a planning-level estimate. Different methodologies give results that vary by 20 percent or more because of aircraft type, load factor and assumptions. The factors are editable on the assumptions tab.

How does it compare with a car or train?

The results chart compares a flight with car, coach and rail for the same journey, assuming ground routes are 20 percent longer than the straight line. Not every route can be travelled by land.

How many trees offset a flight?

A mature tree absorbs roughly 21 kg of CO2 a year, so a 1,000 kg trip equals about 48 tree-years. This is only a rough illustration and does not mean planting trees is a complete offset.

What does carbon offsetting cost?

Prices vary widely, from a few dollars to over a hundred dollars per tonne, depending on the project and its quality. Offsets should supplement, not replace, efforts to cut emissions.

What is a 2-tonne budget?

Many climate scenarios suggest annual per-person emissions need to fall to about 2 tonnes CO2e by mid-century. A single long-haul return flight can use much of that.

Can I add several flights?

Yes. The second tab lets you enter up to six flights with their cabin class and whether they are returns, and totals your yearly flying emissions.

Can I enter my own distance?

Yes. Type a distance in kilometres to use instead of the airports, for routes that are not in the list.

Does it include emissions from making the fuel?

The factors are intended as typical well-to-wake-style planning figures, but methods differ. Treat the numbers as a guide and use the assumptions tab to change them.

Which airports are included?

114 major airports worldwide. You can also use any distance in kilometres.

Is my data stored or sent anywhere?

No. All calculations run in your browser.

Is this flight carbon footprint calculator free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up.
