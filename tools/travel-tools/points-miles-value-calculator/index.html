What this free points and miles value calculator does

You have 60,000 airline miles and a flight that costs $850. Is that a good deal, or should you pay cash and keep the miles? This free online points and miles value calculator answers in one number: cents per point. Enter the cash price, the points required and the taxes and fees you still pay, and it shows what each point is worth, how that compares with your baseline and whether you should redeem or pay cash.

A gauge colours the result from poor to excellent. Further tabs test a rewards card against a flat cash-back card using your spending by category, work out whether buying points during a bonus offer pays off, and compare three redemptions side by side. It works for airline miles, hotel points and flexible bank points, in any currency, and everything runs in your browser.

The cents-per-point formula

The value of a point is the cash you save divided by the points you spend. The cash you save is the cash price minus the taxes and fees you still pay, because those are due either way. Value per point equals (cash price minus fees) divided by points, multiplied by 100 to give cents. For a flight with a cash price of $850, 60,000 miles and $5.60 in fees, that is $844.40 divided by 60,000, which is 1.41 cents per mile. That is above a typical airline baseline of 1.3 cents, so using miles is reasonable.

Points card or cash back?

A rewards card is only better than cash back if the points are worth enough to cover its fee and the cash you could have earned. For the example, spending $400 a month on dining at 3 points per dollar, $300 on travel at 3, $500 on groceries at 2 and $800 elsewhere at 1 earns 14,400 + 10,800 + 12,000 + 9,600 = 46,800 points a year. At 1.3 cents each, that is $608, minus a $95 fee, a net of $513. A flat 2 percent cash-back card on the same $2,000 monthly spend returns $480 a year, so the points card is only $33 ahead. The break-even is the cash-back return plus the fee, $575, divided by the points earned: the points need to be worth about 1.23 cents each to match, and any lower and cash back wins.

Getting more value from points

  • Use points for expensive tickets such as long-haul business class, where cash prices are high.
  • Avoid low-value redemptions such as gift cards or statement credits.
  • Keep an eye on fees and taxes, which can eat the savings.
  • Spend points rather than hoard them, as programmes devalue.
  • Compare with cash, including cheap fares on other airlines.
  • Never carry a balance to earn rewards.

Common mistakes with points and miles

  • Overvaluing points. Using a sky-high value from a blog to justify a poor redemption. Use your own baseline and compare with the cash price.
  • Ignoring taxes and fees. Some awards carry hundreds of dollars in surcharges that reduce the real value per point.
  • Hoarding. Programmes devalue and miles can expire, so a bird in the hand is often better than two in the bush.
  • Spending to earn. Buying things you do not need or carrying a balance to earn rewards costs far more than the rewards are worth.
  • Forgetting alternatives. A cheap cash fare on another airline may beat a mediocre award.
  • Missing the fee. A card with a high annual fee needs enough value to cover it, as the earning tab shows.
  • Not checking availability. Award seats and dynamic prices change, so compare at the time you book.
  • Treating a valuation as a promise. Values depend on the programme, route and date.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the value of a point or mile?

Subtract the taxes and fees you pay in cash from the cash price of the ticket or room, divide by the points required, and multiply by 100 to get cents per point. A flight priced at $850 for 60,000 miles plus $5.60 in fees is worth about 1.41 cents per mile.

What is a good value per point?

It depends on the programme. As a rough planning guide, under 1 cent is poor, 1 to 1.5 is average, 1.5 to 2 is good and over 2 cents is excellent. Hotel points are usually worth less than airline miles.

Why do my points seem worth different amounts?

Value depends on what you redeem for. Business-class flights and expensive hotels often give much higher value per point than gift cards or cash-back redemptions, which are typically a cent or less.

Should I use points or pay cash?

Compare the value you get with your baseline. If the redemption beats your baseline, use points. If not, pay cash and keep the points for a better opportunity.

What is the baseline value?

The cents per point you consider a fair value for the programme. The tool offers starting points for different types of programme, which you can change.

How does the cash-back comparison work?

If a cash-back card gives 2 percent and your points card gives 3 points per dollar, each point has to be worth at least 0.67 cents to beat it. The tool calculates this break-even.

Do taxes and fees matter?

Yes. Many award tickets still charge taxes and surcharges, which can be large. The tool subtracts them from the cash price, since you pay them either way.

What is a devaluation?

When a programme raises the points needed for a reward or lowers earning rates. Points can lose value overnight, so avoid hoarding them for years.

Is it worth buying points?

Sometimes, when there is a large bonus and you have a specific redemption that gives much more value than the cost. The third tab compares the cost per point with the value you expect.

How do I compare a points card with a cash-back card?

The earning tab multiplies your yearly spend by the earn rate in each category, values the points at your baseline, subtracts the annual fee and compares with a flat cash-back card.

Should I include the welcome bonus?

The tab shows year-one value with the bonus and year-two value without, so you can see the long-term picture.

Can I compare more than one redemption?

Yes. The fourth tab compares three options side by side and marks the best value per point.

Do hotel points work the same way?

Yes, enter the room price, the points and any cash fees. Hotel points usually have a lower baseline value.

Are the baselines official valuations?

No. They are rough planning figures that you can change. Programme valuations differ widely between sources and over time.

Should I pay interest to earn points?

No. Interest charges on a card balance almost always cost more than the rewards are worth.

Is my data stored or sent anywhere?

No. All calculations run in your browser.

Is this points and miles calculator free?

Yes. It is free to use online with no sign-up.
