Why eastward travel is harder than westward

Your body's internal clock (circadian rhythm) naturally runs slightly longer than 24 hours, making it easier to delay sleep than to advance it [citation:8]. When you fly east, you need to advance your clock — go to sleep earlier and wake up earlier. This runs against your natural tendency. When you fly west, you need to delay your clock, which feels more natural.

As a rule of thumb, recovery takes about one day per time zone crossed [citation:4][citation:18]. But eastward travel can take longer, roughly 1 hour per day of adjustment, while westward travel adjusts at about 1.5 hours per day [citation:8].

How to beat jet lag: evidence-based tips

Before you travel

  • Start shifting your sleep schedule 2–3 days before departure. If flying east, go to bed 1 hour earlier each night. If flying west, go to bed 1 hour later [citation:8][citation:17].
  • Be well rested. Starting a trip already sleep-deprived makes jet lag worse [citation:18].
  • If your trip is short (under 3 days), consider staying on home time for meals and sleep rather than trying to adapt [citation:2].

During the flight

  • Stay hydrated. Dehydration worsens jet lag symptoms [citation:8].
  • Avoid alcohol and caffeine. Both disrupt sleep and cause dehydration [citation:2][citation:18].
  • Sleep if it's nighttime at your destination. Stay awake if it's daytime there [citation:9].
  • Walk around periodically to reduce stiffness and fatigue [citation:18].

After you arrive

  • Get sunlight during the day. Light is the strongest signal to reset your body clock [citation:4][citation:18].
  • Avoid lengthy daytime naps. Short naps (15–20 minutes) can help alertness without disrupting nighttime sleep [citation:8].
  • Follow the destination's meal and sleep times from day one [citation:2].

Melatonin: how and when to use it

Melatonin can help reset your body clock, but timing is critical. Taking it at the wrong time can make jet lag worse [citation:2][citation:9].

  • Dose: 0.5 mg to 5 mg is typical. Higher doses (5 mg) help you fall asleep faster but aren't more effective for shifting your clock [citation:9].
  • Timing for eastward travel (phase advance): Take melatonin in the afternoon or early evening at destination time. This helps advance your clock. The CDC recommends 0.5 mg about 4.5 hours before your usual sleep onset [citation:10].
  • Timing for westward travel (phase delay): Take melatonin in the morning at destination time if you need to delay your clock [citation:20].
  • Avoid taking melatonin at the wrong time. Morning melatonin can cause sleepiness when you need to be awake [citation:9].

Always consult your doctor before taking melatonin, especially if you take other medications [citation:2].

Frequently asked questions

How long does jet lag last?

On average, recovery takes about one day per time zone crossed. A 6-hour time difference may take roughly 3–6 days to fully adjust, depending on direction and individual factors [citation:4][citation:18].

Is jet lag worse flying east or west?

Eastward travel is generally harder because it requires advancing your body clock, which runs counter to your natural tendency to delay sleep [citation:8].

Does melatonin really work for jet lag?

Yes, studies show melatonin can reduce jet lag symptoms, especially for eastward travel and trips crossing 5+ time zones. But timing is critical — taking it at the wrong time can backfire [citation:9].

What's the best way to reset my sleep schedule after travel?

Get morning sunlight, wake up at a consistent time, and avoid long daytime naps. Your wake-up time is the most powerful anchor for your circadian rhythm [citation:11].
