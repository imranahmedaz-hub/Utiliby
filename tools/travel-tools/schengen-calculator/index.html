What this free online Schengen 90/180 day calculator does

Travelling around Europe on a passport that does not need a visa feels simple until the Schengen 90/180 day rule catches you out. The rule allows non-EU visitors to spend 90 days in any 180-day period inside the Schengen Area. It sounds easy, but because the 180-day window slides forward every single day, working out how many days are left by hand is a common source of mistakes. Digital nomads, long-term travellers, seasonal workers, people with homes in two places and anyone who loves a European summer all run into it.

This free online Schengen calculator takes your list of visits, counts every entry and exit day, and slides the 180-day window across your whole history to find the busiest day. You get days used, days remaining, the last legal day if you stay put, and a table of the earliest date you can come back for stays of 7, 14, 30, 60 or 90 days. A colour-coded 180-day strip and a rolling-count chart show the picture instead of leaving you to trust a number. A second tab plans future trips, and you can add your exit date to your calendar with reminders. Print or save produces a one-page report with only your result, not the whole web page.

Everything runs privately in your browser. Nothing is uploaded, no account is needed, and the optional memory feature stores your stays only on your own device.

The 90 in 180 rule explained in plain words

The rule comes from the Schengen Borders Code. It says that a third-country national who does not need a visa, or who holds a short-stay visa, may stay for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. The phrase any 180-day period is the key. It does not mean a fixed six months that starts on 1 January or on the day you arrive. It means that on every single day of your trip, if you look back at that day and the 179 days before it, you must find 90 or fewer days spent in the area.

Three details cause most mistakes:

  • Partial days count. Arriving at 23:00 or leaving at 01:00 uses a whole day.
  • It is the whole area, not each country. Spending 30 days in Spain, 30 in Italy and 30 in France is 90 days, not 30 in each.
  • Days drop out slowly. A day only leaves the count once it is 180 days old, so a long stay in spring keeps blocking you through the summer.

The calculator checks the rule for every day of your history rather than only today, because an overstay can hide in the middle of a past trip even when today's count looks fine.

Worked example

Imagine a traveller who spends 1 March to 30 April (61 days) in Spain, goes home for a month, and then arrives in Italy on 1 June. On 1 June the 180-day window reaches back to early December, and 61 days are already used. That leaves 29 days. A stay from 1 June can run until 29 June, since the 29 days add up to 90 exactly. Staying one more day would be day 91 and an overstay.

Now suppose the same traveller waits until 1 September. The window starts in early March, so the first days of the Spain stay have already dropped out and more days are free. The earlier you plan, the more days come back, which is why the earliest-entry table matters for planning a return.

Which countries count towards the 90 days

The Schengen Area has no internal border checks among its 29 members. Time in any of them adds to the same pool. Bulgaria and Romania became full members at the start of 2025, when land border checks with them ended, and Croatia joined in 2023.

Who the rule applies to, and who it does not

The rule applies to citizens of countries whose passport holders can enter Schengen without a visa, such as the United States, the UK, Canada, Australia, Japan and many others, and to people holding a short-stay (Type C) Schengen visa. A multi-entry visa still follows the same 90/180 limit.

It does not apply to citizens of the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland, to their family members under free movement rules, or to people living in a Schengen country on a residence permit or a national long-stay visa. Time spent on such documents does not count towards the 90 days. If you are unsure which category you fall under, ask the embassy or consulate of the country you plan to visit.

Digital nomads and remote workers

The 90/180 rule limits how long you can stay as a visitor. It does not make working legal. Several countries now offer digital nomad or long-stay visas that sit outside the short-stay count. If you hold one, keep the document with you and track it separately.

EES, ETIAS and how border records work

The Entry/Exit System (EES) is a digital border register that replaced manual passport stamping for non-EU short-stay visitors. At the first crossing you provide a photo and fingerprints, and later each entry and exit is logged. The system calculates your days for the officer, which means your travel record is exact and an overstay shows up automatically. Because the record is digital, you should keep your own log. This tool helps you do that and compare it with what you expect.

ETIAS is a separate, planned online travel authorisation for visitors who do not need a visa. It is a pre-travel check, not a stay permit, and it does not change the 90/180 limit. At the time of writing it has not started. Official guidance will state when it becomes mandatory, and the approved authorisation will be tied to your passport.

Whatever the border system shows is what officers rely on, so if your count and the border record ever disagree, ask the border authority how it was calculated before you leave.

Planning a trip without overstaying

  • Test the whole trip. Enter the planned dates in the Plan a trip tab. The tool checks the count on every day of the stay, not only the last one.
  • Find the longest stay. Enter only the entry date to see how many days that start allows and the last legal day.
  • Find the earliest return. Use the table on the first tab to see when a stay of 7, 14, 30, 60 or 90 days becomes possible again.
  • Split trips wisely. Because days drop out gradually, two 45-day visits separated by a gap can fit where one 90-day visit followed by an immediate return cannot.
  • Leave a buffer. A cancelled flight or a strike can delay your exit. Plan to leave two or three days before your last legal day.
  • Set a reminder. Download the calendar file for your last legal day. It adds alerts seven days and one day before.

Common mistakes the calculator helps you avoid

  • Counting from a fixed date such as 1 January instead of a rolling window.
  • Not counting the entry day or the exit day.
  • Treating each country as having its own 90 days.
  • Assuming you can leave for one night and reset the count. A short exit does not reset anything.
  • Forgetting trips from many months ago that still sit inside the 180 days.
  • Counting time in Ireland, Cyprus or the UK.
  • Mixing up the 90/180 rule with the 'Schengen visa validity' printed on a visa sticker. They are different limits and both apply.

What happens if you overstay

An overstay is staying longer than your permitted days. Even a few days can have an effect. Penalties vary by country and may include a fine on departure, a longer ban on re-entry, a note in the border database that can affect later visa and ETIAS decisions, and in serious cases detention and deportation. Some countries are more lenient on short overstays caused by emergencies, but you should not rely on that. If you are already over the limit, contact the immigration authority or a qualified adviser before you leave, and keep evidence of the reason, such as medical records or cancelled transport.

Frequently asked questions

How does the Schengen 90/180 day rule work?

In any period of 180 days you may spend at most 90 days in the Schengen Area on a short stay. The 180 days are a rolling window ending on the date you check, not a calendar half-year, so the count changes every day.

Do the day of entry and the day of exit count?

Yes. Both the day you enter and the day you leave count as full days, even if you were there for only a few hours. A stay from 1 to 10 March is 10 days, not 9.

Is the 180-day period fixed or rolling?

Rolling. For any date, look back 179 days plus that date itself. Days older than that no longer count. This is why a single reset date does not exist.

How do I find out how many days I have left?

Add every past stay, set the check date to today and read the days remaining. The tool also shows the last legal day if you stay without a break.

How long must I stay out before coming back?

There is no fixed waiting time. You can return as soon as the rolling count lets you. After a full 90-day stay you generally need to wait about 90 days to be allowed a new 90-day stay. The table in the tool shows exact earliest entry dates.

Which countries are in the Schengen Area?

Twenty-nine countries: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Italy, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland. Ireland and Cyprus are in the EU but not in Schengen.

Do days in Ireland, Cyprus or the UK count?

No. Only time spent inside the Schengen Area counts. Ireland, Cyprus, the UK, Albania, Serbia and other non-Schengen countries do not use up days.

Do days in the microstates count?

Monaco, San Marino and the Vatican have open borders with their neighbours and are treated as part of the Schengen Area, so time there counts. Andorra is not in Schengen, but you must cross France or Spain to reach it.

Does the rule apply to everyone?

No. It applies to short stays of non-EU citizens who do not need a visa or who hold a short-stay Schengen visa. EU, EEA and Swiss citizens, and people with a residence permit or long-stay national visa, follow different rules.

Can a long-stay visa or residence permit change the count?

Time spent on a national long-stay visa or residence permit of a Schengen state does not count towards the 90 days for short stays. Always check with the issuing country.

What happens if I overstay?

Consequences range from a warning to fines, deportation and an entry ban of up to several years, and an overstay can lead to later visa refusals. The penalty depends on the country and on how long you overstayed.

What is the Entry/Exit System (EES)?

EES is a digital border system that registers non-EU visitors with fingerprints and a photo and records entry and exit dates automatically. It replaces passport stamps, so officers can see your exact days. It has been in operation since late 2025 and was fully rolled out in April 2026.

What is ETIAS and do I need it?

ETIAS is a planned online travel authorisation for visa-exempt travellers, similar to a US ESTA. It has not started yet at the time of writing. When it does it will not change the 90/180 rule. Check the official EU website for the start date.

Can I use the calculator for future trips?

Yes. Use the Plan a trip tab to enter a planned entry date and see the longest stay allowed, or enter an exit date to test a specific trip. You can also add planned trips in the first tab.

Do some countries allow extra days through bilateral agreements?

A few older bilateral visa agreements let citizens of certain countries stay longer in specific Schengen states, in addition to the 90 days. These are rare and nationality specific, and this tool does not apply them.

Is my data stored or sent anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser. If you tick the remember box, your stays are saved only in this browser on this device, and you can clear them any time with Reset.

Is this the same as the official EU calculator?

The method is the same: a 180-day look-back window with entry and exit days counted. The official short-stay calculator from the European Commission is a good second check before you travel.
