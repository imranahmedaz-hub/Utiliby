What is a free passport validity checker online?

A free passport validity checker online is a calculator that compares your passport dates with the entry rules of the country you plan to visit. Passport validity rules are not universal. Some countries want six months of validity, some want three, some only require the passport to be valid on the days you are there, and the Schengen area adds a limit on how old the passport can be. Working out which rule applies, and then counting the months correctly, is exactly the kind of small calculation that goes wrong the night before a flight.

This tool does that work for you. You enter the passport issue and expiry dates, choose a destination and add your arrival and departure dates. It applies the rule for that destination, shows the exact date the passport must remain valid until, tells you how many days you have to spare and draws a timeline. It goes further than a basic 6-month calculator: it handles several stops on one trip, up to eight travellers at once, a custom rule for destinations that are not listed, and a second tab that turns your expiry date into a list of key dates and the last day you can travel to every country.

It is built for people searching for an answer such as is my passport valid for Thailand, does my passport need six months for Schengen, passport expiry date calculator for travel or can I travel with a passport that expires in five months. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is uploaded, and there is no account to create.

The 6-month passport rule

The six-month rule asks that your passport remains valid for at least six months after your trip. It exists partly so that a traveller who is delayed, falls ill or overstays still holds a valid document, and partly because it is simple for border staff and airlines to enforce. It is common across Asia, the Middle East, Africa and Latin America, and it is the most frequently misunderstood rule because countries describe it differently. Some say six months from the date of entry, others six months beyond the end of your stay, and travel guides often disagree about the same country.

When sources disagree, this checker takes the stricter reading, counts from your departure date, and marks the destination with a note so you know to verify it. That choice never produces a false pass. If you have official wording that counts from arrival, switch the Options box to Arrival date. The passport must then still cover your whole stay, so the requirement is the later of arrival plus six months and your departure date.

Practical examples: a passport that expires on 10 March 2027 fails for a trip to Thailand that ends on 15 November 2026, because six months after departure is 15 May 2027. It would pass for the same dates in Japan, which only requires the passport to cover the stay. The same document would clear the Schengen three-month test, which needs it to last until 15 February 2027.

Schengen: 3 months plus the 10-year rule

The Schengen area has no internal border checks between its member states, and one set of passport rules applies to visitors who need no visa. Under the Schengen Borders Code your passport must be valid for at least three months after the date you intend to leave the Schengen area, and it must have been issued within the previous ten years. The second condition is the one that catches people out. Many passports used to carry unused months over from the previous one when renewed early, so a passport could be issued more than ten years ago and still not have expired. Such a passport can be refused at the border.

The figure shows a real-looking case: a passport issued on 1 July 2016 and expiring on 30 March 2027. A traveller arriving in Spain on 20 August 2026 appears to have seven months left, yet the passport is already past its tenth birthday, so entry fails. The checker flags this with the issue-date test, states the date the ten-year limit was reached, and tells you that a passport issued on or after 20 Aug 2016 would satisfy the rule for that arrival. A newly issued passport always does.

Leave the issue date empty and the Schengen result becomes CHECK rather than a pass, because the second half of the rule has not been tested. Two blank pages are also commonly asked for. If your trip crosses several Schengen countries, the three months are counted from your last exit from the area, so use the Schengen Area (any member) option or the country you leave from.

Visa-exempt visitors to the Schengen area are also due to need an ETIAS travel authorisation, expected to begin from late 2026 according to recent reports. ETIAS is linked to the passport used in the application, so a renewed passport needs a new authorisation. It sits alongside the passport rules above rather than replacing them. United States and the six-month club

For the United States the general rule is a passport valid for six months beyond the period of intended stay. Citizens of countries on the so-called six-month club list are exempt, and only need a passport valid for the stay. US Customs and Border Protection published a revised exempt list in a Carrier Liaison Program bulletin on 18 December 2025, which covers more than 100 countries and replaces earlier versions. Because the answer depends on nationality, the checker offers two United States options: exempt nationality (valid for the stay) and non-exempt nationality (six months beyond the stay). Pick the one that matches your passport, and check the current list on the CBP or State Department website.

Countries that only need a passport valid for the stay

A number of countries only require that your passport covers the time you spend there. This is the easiest rule to meet, but it is not a licence to travel on a nearly expired document. Airlines and cruise lines often ask for more, and a delayed return flight can leave you abroad with an expired passport. The checker shows an airline caution whenever a stay-only country is chosen and your passport has under six months left after departure.

How the dates are calculated

All dates are handled as whole calendar days, so time zones and daylight saving never move a result by a day. Months are added on the calendar, not as 30-day blocks: six months after 15 August is 15 February. When the target month is shorter, the date is clamped to the last day of that month, so six months after 31 August is 28 February (29 in a leap year). A passport that expires on the required date passes with zero days to spare, which the checker shows as PASS – TIGHT; an expiry one day earlier fails.

For a rule counted from departure the required date is departure plus the buffer. For a rule counted from arrival it is the later of arrival plus the buffer and the departure date. A stay-only rule requires validity through departure, and a valid-on-arrival rule requires it on the arrival date. Your optional safety margin is added on top. Schengen adds the issue test: arrival must fall on or before the date ten years after issue.

Airlines, cruise lines and Timatic

Airlines are responsible for the documents of the passengers they carry and can be fined if a passenger is refused entry, so they check passports at the desk. Most use the IATA Timatic database, which can be stricter than the rule a border officer would apply. This is why a destination that technically accepts a passport valid only for your stay can still see you refused at check-in if you have under six months left. Cruise lines commonly apply a six-month policy to every passenger. The sensible approach is to treat this checker as a first filter, then ask your airline or cruise line what it will accept, and keep their reply.

Pre-flight passport checklist

  • Check the expiry date against the rule for every country on your route, including transit points.
  • For Schengen, confirm the passport was issued within the last ten years on arrival day.
  • Count the blank pages and make sure the passport is not torn, water-damaged or altered.
  • Confirm the visa, eTA or ETIAS you need, and that it is linked to the passport you will carry.
  • Make sure the name on your tickets matches the passport exactly.
  • Ask your airline or cruise line whether it applies a stricter validity rule.
  • Keep a photo of the passport photo page and your visa documents in a safe place.

Renewal timing and apply-by dates

Passport processing times differ by country, by season and by whether you pay for a faster service. Standard handling often takes several weeks, and peak travel seasons can add to the wait. The checker lets you set your renewal time in weeks, defaulting to eight, and calculates the date to apply by from the first arrival that would fail. If that date has already passed, it says so and suggests asking about expedited service or changing the dates. Remember that a new passport is a different document: visas, ETIAS authorisations and some airline bookings may need updating to the new number.

Also note that a passport issued for a child is often valid for a shorter period than an adult passport, for example five years in several countries. Family trips are where the unexpected expiry usually appears, which is why the checker accepts several travellers at once.

Families, multi-stop trips, transit and cruises

Families and groups. Add each traveller with their own issue and expiry dates. The grid shows every traveller against every stop, with the weakest passport highlighted, and each person gets their own apply-by date and timeline.

Multi-stop trips. Enter each country as a stop with its own dates. The passport has to satisfy every stop, so the required-until date is driven by the strictest one. The fix box gives a single minimum expiry date that covers the whole itinerary.

Transit and layovers. Some countries apply their validity rule to passengers in transit, and a layover that needs you to leave the airport is an entry. Add the transit country as a stop with the layover dates if you are unsure.

Cruises. The cruise line decides what it accepts, and many ask for six months after the cruise ends regardless of the ports visited. Use the Custom rule with six months counted from departure, or add a safety margin.

Common passport validity mistakes

  • Assuming one rule fits every country. Stay-only, three-month and six-month rules all exist, sometimes on the same trip.
  • Counting from the wrong day. Counting from arrival when the embassy means departure can turn a pass into a fail on a long trip.
  • Forgetting the Schengen issue date. A passport that has not expired can still be too old.
  • Ignoring the airline. The carrier may apply a stricter policy than the border.
  • Leaving renewal too late. Processing time, not the expiry date, is the real deadline.
  • Not checking transit points. A connecting country may have its own rule.
  • Mixing up the ETIAS or eTA with the passport. Authorisations are tied to the passport number, so a renewal means a new application.
