What is a free closed caption generator online?

A free closed caption generator online is a tool that creates time-coded text files, usually in SRT or WebVTT format, that display the spoken words and relevant sounds of a video as on-screen text. Captions are called "closed" because viewers can turn them on or off, unlike "open" captions that are permanently burned into the video.

This closed caption generator is an editor. It does not listen to your video and transcribe it. Instead it gives you a complete authoring environment: add cues, type or paste text, set precise start and end times, add speaker names and sound-effect notes, and check everything against WCAG-based and industry captioning rules before you export. Nothing is uploaded, so scripts and unpublished videos stay on your device.

Closed captions, open captions and subtitles

Closed captions transcribe the dialogue and the other audio a viewer needs, such as speaker identification, sound effects and music, and viewers can switch them on or off. Open captions are part of the picture and cannot be turned off. Subtitles usually translate dialogue into another language and assume the viewer can hear the sound.

All three can be authored from the same kind of file. The practical difference is content: a subtitle track holds the words, while a caption track holds the words plus the sounds and speaker information that a deaf or hard of hearing viewer would otherwise miss.

WCAG caption requirements

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) set the legal and practical baseline for video accessibility in many countries. The criteria that concern captions are:

  • SC 1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded), Level A: captions are provided for all prerecorded audio content in synchronized media, except when the media is a clearly labelled alternative for text.
  • SC 1.2.4 Captions (Live), Level AA: captions are provided for live audio content in synchronized media.
  • SC 1.2.5 Audio Description (Prerecorded), Level AA: an audio description is provided for prerecorded video content. This is separate from captions and describes what is happening on screen.

WCAG does not set exact line lengths or reading speeds. Those come from style guides used by broadcasters, streaming services and captioning programmes. This tool applies commonly used limits so that your captions are not only present but readable. A file that passes these checks is a good draft, but it is not a certificate of WCAG conformance.

How to time and break captions

Good timing matters as much as good wording. These habits make captions feel natural:

  • Start with the speech. Show a caption as the speaker begins, not several words later.
  • Leave a moment at the end. Keep the caption up for a short time after the speech stops so the viewer can finish reading, unless the next caption is due.
  • Break at natural phrases. Split lines and cues at punctuation or between clauses. Avoid ending a line on "the", "a" or "of".
  • Balance two-line cues. Two lines of similar length are easier to read than a long line over a short one. The Auto-split option balances lines for you.
  • Keep one idea per cue. If a sentence needs more than two lines, split it over two cues at a sensible point.
  • Match the cut. Where possible, avoid a caption that stays on screen across a hard cut to a new scene.

The Auto-split option shares your chosen duration between the cues in proportion to their length, which is a sensible starting point. It cannot hear your audio, so always check the result in the Video Preview tab and adjust the times.

Speaker names and sound effects

Closed captions describe meaningful audio, not just speech. Use short notes in square brackets or parentheses and keep them in the present tense:

SituationExample caption
Speaker off screenMaria: Where were you?
Sound that matters to the story[door slams]
Music[soft piano music]
Tone or delivery that changes the meaning[sarcastically] Great idea.
Audience reaction[laughter]

Do not describe sounds the viewer can already understand from the picture, and avoid describing every background noise. In the editor, type the speaker's name in the Speaker box and the tool formats it for you: Name: text in SRT and a <v Name> voice tag in VTT.

SRT vs VTT: which format to use

SRT (SubRip) is the oldest and most widely supported caption format. Almost every video player, editing program and social platform accepts it. A cue is just a number, a time range and text. Timestamps use a comma before the milliseconds, for example 00:00:01,000.

VTT (WebVTT) is the W3C standard for captions on the web. The file begins with WEBVTT and supports voice tags, positioning and CSS styling. Timestamps use a dot before the milliseconds, for example 00:00:01.000.

Rule of thumb: choose SRT for maximum compatibility with video platforms and editing software, and VTT when the video plays on your own website through an HTML5 <track> element or when you need voice tags and styling. If you are unsure, export both.

How to use your caption file

  • YouTube: in YouTube Studio open your video, choose Subtitles, add a language and upload the SRT file with timing.
  • Your own website: add <track kind="captions" src="captions.vtt" srclang="en" label="English" default> inside your <video> element.
  • Video editors: most editing programs can import an SRT file as a caption or subtitle track.
  • Course platforms and learning systems: check which format your platform accepts. Many take both SRT and VTT.
  • Social platforms: several accept an SRT upload, and others burn captions into the picture through their own tools.

Platform menus change from time to time, so use their current help pages for the exact steps.

Common caption mistakes

  • Captions that are too fast. Look for red CPS values and split or shorten the cue.
  • Overlapping times. Two cues sharing a moment are an error. End one cue before the next begins.
  • Missing sounds and speakers. If the story depends on an off-screen voice or a sound, say so.
  • Autogenerated text left uncorrected. Automatic speech recognition makes mistakes with names and technical words. Always proofread, then use this editor to fix the text and the timing.
  • Captions placed over important text. Check the preview for on-screen titles that a caption might cover.
  • Forgetting to export. This tool does not save your work, so download your file before you leave.

Why captions matter

Captions are first an access feature. They let people who are deaf or hard of hearing follow the video, and they are a legal or policy requirement for many schools, employers and public bodies. They also help many other viewers: people watching without sound in public places, people learning a language, people in noisy rooms, and anyone who finds it easier to follow spoken content in writing. A caption file is also text that search tools and transcripts can use, which makes the video easier to find and quote.

Frequently asked questions

What is a closed caption generator?

A closed caption generator is a tool that creates time-coded text files (SRT, VTT) that accompany a video and display the spoken words and relevant sound effects as on-screen text. Captions are called 'closed' because viewers can turn them on or off. This free closed caption generator online supports manual cue authoring, SRT and WebVTT export, WCAG-based checks and video preview.

What is the difference between subtitles and closed captions?

Subtitles translate spoken dialogue into another language and assume the viewer can hear the audio. Closed captions describe all significant audio (dialogue, speaker identification, sound effects, music and tone) and are designed for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing. In practice, both use the same SRT and VTT file formats.

What are the WCAG caption requirements?

WCAG 2.1 Success Criterion 1.2.2 (Level A) requires synchronized captions for all pre-recorded audio in multimedia. Success Criterion 1.2.4 (Level AA) requires captions for live media. WCAG does not set exact line lengths or reading speeds, so this tool also checks common best practice: about 42 characters per line or fewer, no more than two lines, reading speeds under 20 characters per second, and cue durations between 1 and 8 seconds.

What is SRT format?

SRT (SubRip Subtitle) is the most widely supported caption format. Each cue has a sequential number, a time range (start --> end), and text on one or more lines, with a blank line separating cues. Timestamps use the format HH:MM:SS,mmm with a comma before the milliseconds.

What is VTT format?

VTT (Web Video Text Tracks, also called WebVTT) is the W3C standard format for web video captions. It starts with the text WEBVTT and uses timestamps in the format HH:MM:SS.mmm with a dot before the milliseconds. VTT supports styling with CSS, cue identifiers, and voice spans via <v Speaker> tags.

What is a good reading speed for captions?

A common ceiling for adult content is 20 characters per second (CPS), and 15 to 17 CPS is more comfortable for most viewers. Children's content should aim lower. This closed caption generator flags any cue above 20 CPS and shows the CPS of every cue in green, amber or red.

What line length should captions use?

Most guidelines keep captions to two lines of roughly 32 to 42 characters each. Streaming style guides commonly use 42 characters as the upper limit, and some broadcasters use slightly shorter lines. This tool flags any line over 42 characters and any cue with more than two lines.

How do I time captions accurately?

Load your video in the Video Preview tab, play it, and watch where each caption appears. Start a caption when the speech starts and end it shortly after the speech ends. Give every caption at least one second on screen, avoid overlaps, and change the start and end boxes until the overlay matches the audio. What time formats can I type into the start and end boxes?

You can type 00:00:01.500, 00:00:01,500, 1:30 (minutes and seconds), 90 (seconds), 90.5 or a style such as 1m30s. The tool converts what you type into the standard timestamp when it exports.

How do I add a caption file to a YouTube video?

Download the SRT file, open YouTube Studio, choose Subtitles for your video, add a language, then choose Upload file and With timing. Pick the SRT file and publish. YouTube's own menus change from time to time, so follow the on-screen labels if they differ.

How do I add captions to my own website video?

Download the VTT file and add a track element inside your video tag, for example: <track kind="captions" src="captions.vtt" srclang="en" label="English" default>. The browser then offers a captions button in the video controls. Serve the VTT file from the same site, or enable cross-origin access if it is on another domain.

What is the difference between open and closed captions?

Closed captions are a separate track that viewers can switch on or off. Open captions are burned into the picture and cannot be turned off. This tool produces closed caption files. Open captions need a video editor that draws the text onto the frames.

Should I include speaker names and sound descriptions?

For captions, yes, when they are needed to follow the video. Name a speaker when they are off screen or when it is not obvious who is talking, and describe meaningful sounds in square brackets, such as [door slams] or [applause]. Subtitles for hearing viewers usually leave these out. Use the Speaker box in each cue and this tool will add the name in the correct format for SRT or VTT.

Does the tool save my captions?

Is there a limit on how many cues I can create?

The tool does not set a limit. Everything is drawn on the page and kept in your browser's memory, so a very long track with thousands of cues may feel slower than a short one. For long videos, work on one section at a time and join the exported files afterwards.
