What this free online business valuation calculator does

Whether you are thinking of selling, buying, bringing in a partner or simply wondering what you have built, the first question is the same: what is this business worth? Professional valuations are slow and expensive, and many online calculators give a single number with no explanation. This free online business valuation calculator gives you a range, shows how it was built, and lets you see which factors help or hurt.

It starts with the multiple that businesses in your industry typically sell for, then adjusts it for the things buyers actually care about: how much of your revenue is recurring, how fast you are growing, how dependent you are on one customer, how much depends on you as the owner, and your size. It then cross-checks the answer against a revenue multiple, a discounted cash flow and your net assets, and turns enterprise value into equity value by adding cash and subtracting debt.

A football-field chart shows every method on one scale, a waterfall chart shows how each driver moves the midpoint, and a grid shows value at other multiples and earnings. You can print or save a clean one-page report. Everything runs privately in your browser.

How to use this business valuation tool

  1. Choose your industry from 21 sectors. This sets the starting multiple range.
  2. Enter revenue and earnings. Use EBITDA, or seller's earnings for an owner-run business.
  3. Set the value drivers: recurring revenue, growth, your largest customer and how dependent the business is on you.
  4. Add the balance sheet: net tangible assets, cash and debt.
  5. Review the range. The headline shows equity value, with the multiple and the cross-checks beside it.
  6. Open Value drivers to see what you could improve, and the grid to test other multiples.

What pushes a multiple up or down

  • Recurring revenue. Income that repeats is worth much more than income you must win again every year.
  • Growth. A business growing 30% a year commands a clear premium over one growing 3%.
  • Customer concentration. Above 20% from one customer, buyers discount. Above 40%, some walk away.
  • Owner dependence. A business that runs without you is easier to sell and to finance.
  • Gross margin. Higher margin revenue is worth more per unit.
  • Clean financials. Accountant-prepared accounts remove doubt and speed up a deal.
  • Size. Larger earnings attract more buyers, so multiples rise with scale.

The calculator turns the first five of these into a transparent percentage adjustment you can see on the Value drivers tab.

EBITDA is not cash flow

EBITDA ignores interest, tax and the cost of replacing equipment. A factory with strong EBITDA but heavy capital spending generates far less cash than the headline suggests. That is why the tool also shows a discounted cash flow based on a cash conversion rate you set. If the DCF is far below the multiple-based value, look at capital spending and working capital.

From enterprise value to what the owner receives

The earnings methods give enterprise value, the value of the operating business. To estimate what an owner takes home, add the cash that stays with the seller and subtract debt that must be repaid at closing. Deals also assume a normal level of working capital, taxes on sale vary by country, and fees for brokers and advisers come off the top. This tool handles the cash and debt bridge, so remember to allow for fees and tax separately.

Common valuation mistakes

  • Applying an EBITDA multiple to net profit, or an SDE multiple to EBITDA.
  • Using a national average multiple instead of an industry and size specific one.
  • Not normalising owner pay, perks and one-off items.
  • Ignoring working capital and debt.
  • Treating the midpoint as a price rather than a range.
  • Confusing an estimate with an offer. A sale needs a buyer.

Preparing a business for sale or valuation

Value is not only a matter of numbers. Buyers pay more for businesses that look predictable and independent of the owner. Clean financial statements for at least three years, recurring or contracted revenue, a diversified customer base, documented processes and a management team that can run the business all raise the multiple. Cutting one-off costs and removing personal expenses from the accounts also shows the true earnings. Use the driver tab of the calculator to see which of these factors move your estimate most.

Frequently asked questions

What is a business valuation calculator?

A tool that estimates what a business might sell for. This one multiplies your earnings by an industry range, adjusts it for risk, and checks the result against revenue, discounted cash flow and asset methods.

How do you value a small business?

Most small businesses are valued as a multiple of earnings. Owner-operated firms use seller's discretionary earnings, larger firms use EBITDA. Revenue multiples, discounted cash flow and net assets are used as cross-checks.

What is EBITDA?

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It approximates operating profit before financing and accounting choices. It is not the same as cash flow.

What is seller's discretionary earnings (SDE)?

Net profit plus the owner's pay and perks, interest, depreciation and one-off costs. It shows what a single owner-operator could earn from the business.

What EBITDA multiple should I use?

It depends on sector and size. Small owner-run businesses often sell for two to four times earnings, established firms four to eight, and software or high-growth firms more. The tool picks a range by industry, then adjusts it.

Why does recurring revenue raise the value?

Because buyers pay more for income that repeats without being re-won. Subscriptions and long contracts reduce risk.

Why does customer concentration lower the value?

If one customer is a large share of revenue, losing them after the sale could wreck the business, so buyers discount the price or walk away.

What does owner dependence mean?

How much the business relies on you personally. A business that runs without the owner is worth more than one that stops when the owner leaves.

What is the difference between enterprise value and equity value?

Enterprise value is the value of the operating business. Equity value adds the cash and subtracts the debt, which is closer to what an owner receives.

How does the DCF cross-check work?

It projects free cash flow for five years, fades growth towards a long-term rate, and discounts the cash flows and a terminal value at your discount rate. A rate of 15% to 25% is common for small businesses.

Why is net asset value shown?

It is a floor. If earnings-based value is below the assets, a buyer may prefer to buy the assets directly.

Can I value a business with no profit?

The tool falls back to a revenue multiple, but this is much less reliable. Buyers of unprofitable firms look at growth, margins and the path to profit.

How accurate is the estimate?

Treat it as a starting range. Two sensible buyers can differ by 30% or more. A broker or appraiser can test it against actual sales.

Should I use the midpoint?

Use the range. A quick private sale often achieves less than an open process with several bidders.

What costs should I normalise before valuing?

Remove one-off items, owner perks, above-market related-party rent and unusual income, and add a market salary for the owner if they work in the business.

Does working capital matter?

Yes. Most deals assume a normal level of receivables, inventory and payables comes with the business. Stripping it changes the real price.

Is my data stored?

No. The calculator runs in your browser and nothing is saved or sent.
