What this free online startup runway calculator does

Cash is the oxygen of a young company. Profit can wait, product-market fit can wait, but when the bank balance reaches zero the business stops. The number that tells you how long you have is your runway, and every founder, finance lead and investor watches it closely.

This free online startup runway calculator goes beyond cash divided by burn. Enter your cash, monthly expenses and revenue, plus how fast each is growing, and it projects your balance month by month for up to five years. It marks the month cash runs out, the date you should start fundraising, the break-even month and your lowest cash point. Two live charts show cash under three burn scenarios and revenue against expenses, and a scenario table shows how sensitive the answer is to cost and revenue changes. You can add a one-off expense, a new hire and an expected funding round to see their effect.

How to use this startup runway calculator

  1. Pick a stage preset if you want a starting point, or type your own numbers.
  2. Enter cash on hand you can spend on operations.
  3. Enter monthly expenses and revenue. Use gross burn, including payroll, rent, tools, marketing and loan payments.
  4. Set growth rates for revenue and expenses, or use zero for a flat projection.
  5. Add events such as a big purchase, a hiring plan or a funding round.
  6. Set your raise lead time. Nine months is a common planning figure.
  7. Read the result, compare scenarios and print the report for your board or investors.

Runway formulas and definitions

Gross burn is everything you spend per month. Net burn is gross burn minus revenue. Simple runway is cash divided by net burn. The projected runway in this tool steps forward one month at a time, so growth, hires and funding change the answer. The fundraising date is the cash-out date minus your lead time.

Worked example

A seed-stage SaaS company has 600,000 in cash, spends 80,000 a month and earns 30,000. Net burn is 50,000, so simple runway is 12 months.

How to extend your runway

  • Cut non-essential spend, such as unused software, travel and low-return marketing.
  • Raise prices or move customers to annual plans for upfront cash.
  • Collect faster. Shorten invoice terms and chase overdue bills.
  • Delay hires until revenue supports them.
  • Use non-dilutive funding, such as grants, revenue-based financing or a business loan.
  • Negotiate costs, including rent and vendor contracts.

Common runway mistakes

  • Counting revenue you have not collected.
  • Forgetting taxes, annual subscriptions and benefits that arrive in lumps.
  • Assuming a funding round will close on a fixed date.
  • Using optimistic growth rates without a downside case.
  • Not updating after hires or lost customers.

Burn rate, runway and related startup metrics

Runway sits among a family of cash metrics. Gross burn shows how expensive the company is to run. Net burn shows how fast the bank balance falls. The burn multiple, net burn divided by new annual recurring revenue, shows how efficiently spending turns into growth, and a figure under 2 is usually considered healthy. Default alive means that at current growth and spending the company reaches profitability before it runs out of cash. Default dead means it does not. The break-even month and cash-out date in this tool give you a quick read on which side you are on.

Investors look at runway together with growth. A company with 24 months of cash and flat revenue is less attractive than one with 12 months and revenue doubling every year, because the second has a clear path to the next round. Use the revenue growth input honestly: a rate you have sustained for six months is credible, a rate you hope for is not.

Planning your fundraising timeline

Raising money takes longer than most founders expect. Preparing materials, building a list of investors, first meetings, partner meetings, due diligence and legal work often add up to six to nine months, and rounds that look certain can slip. That is why the tool subtracts a lead time from your cash-out date to give a start-raising date.

  • Start early. Raising from a position of strength gives better terms than raising in a panic.
  • Show momentum. Investors respond to growth, so time your raise after a strong quarter.
  • Keep a plan B. Know which costs you would cut and what a bridge from existing investors would look like.
  • Never count unsigned money. Enter funding only when a term sheet is signed.

Using the scenarios to make decisions

The scenario tab turns what-if questions into numbers. If you are deciding whether to hire three engineers, test burn at 20% and 50% higher and see how many months you give up. If you are negotiating a large customer, test revenue 30% higher. If cash is tight, test burn 20% lower to see how much runway a modest cost cut buys. Because each row changes only one thing, you can see which lever matters most and decide where to act first.

Accuracy, limits and responsible use

The projection assumes steady monthly growth and the figures you enter. Real businesses have seasonality, payment delays and surprises, so keep a buffer and use the scenario table. This tool is for planning, not a substitute for a finance model built from your accounts.

Frequently asked questions

What is startup runway?

The number of months your company can keep operating before cash reaches zero, given your current spending and income.

How do I calculate runway?

Divide cash on hand by monthly net burn. With 600,000 in the bank and 50,000 of net burn, runway is 12 months. This tool also projects growth month by month for a more realistic date.

What is the difference between gross burn and net burn?

Gross burn is total monthly spending. Net burn is gross burn minus the revenue you collect. Runway uses net burn.

How many months of runway should a startup have?

Many investors suggest at least 12 to 18 months after a raise, and never letting it fall below six months before you act. A longer runway gives you room to negotiate.

When should I start fundraising?

Start when you have 9 to 12 months of runway left, since raising often takes 6 to 9 months. The tool shows a start-by date based on your lead time.

What does a short runway mean?

Under six months is critical: you may need to cut costs, bridge, or take worse terms. The tool colors your result so it is obvious.

How does revenue growth change runway?

Faster revenue growth shrinks net burn each month and extends runway, sometimes reaching break-even. Slower growth or rising costs shorten it.

What is the break-even month?

The first month in which monthly revenue equals or exceeds monthly expenses. After that, your cash stops falling on its own.

Why is the simple runway different from the projected runway?

The simple figure assumes burn stays flat. The projection applies your growth rates, hires, one-off costs and funding, so it is usually more accurate.

How do I model a new hire?

Enter the extra monthly cost and the month it starts. The runway chart updates, and you can compare the result with the base case.

Can I include an expected funding round?

Yes. Enter the amount and the month it arrives. Remember to set it only when money is likely, not just hoped for.

What are the scenarios for?

They rerun your figures with burn up 20% or 50%, burn down 20%, and revenue up or down 30%. They show how sensitive your runway is.

Should I include debt repayments?

Yes. Add loan payments and any other regular cash outflows to monthly expenses so net burn reflects real cash leaving the bank.

Is runway the same as profit?

No. Runway is about cash. You can be unprofitable and well funded, or profitable and short of cash if customers pay late.

What if my revenue is lumpy?

Use a trailing three-month average, or run the scenarios with lower and higher revenue to cover the range.

How often should I update runway?

Monthly, after your books close. Update it any time you hire, raise prices, sign a large customer or lose one.

Is my data stored?

No. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is sent or saved.
