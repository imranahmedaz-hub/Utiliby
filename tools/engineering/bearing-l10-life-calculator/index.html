What Does L10 Bearing Life Mean?

L10 is a statistical rolling-contact fatigue rating: under the rating model, 90% of a sufficiently large population of apparently identical bearings are expected to reach or exceed that life before fatigue occurs. It is also called basic rating life or B10 life in some engineering contexts. It is not the mean life, the maximum life, or a promise that one installed bearing will survive for that duration. Individual bearings and real installations can fail earlier or run longer.

The calculator distinguishes L10 in million revolutions from L10h in operating hours. A result of 64 million revolutions means 64,000,000 revolutions, not 64 hours. Conversion to hours requires a positive rotational speed. Schedule conversions additionally require your hours per day and operating days per year. Scheduled years are a planning representation of the calculated fatigue hours, not an approved replacement interval.

The Basic Bearing Life Formula

L10 = (C / P)p million revolutions
L10h = 1,000,000 × (C / P)p / (60 × n)
  • C: basic dynamic load rating from the bearing manufacturer's catalogue.
  • P: equivalent dynamic bearing load for the application.
  • n: rotational speed in revolutions per minute, RPM.
  • p: 3 for ball bearings, or 10/3 for roller bearings.

C and P must use the same force unit. This calculator accepts newtons, kilonewtons, and pounds-force. Pounds-force is a force, not a mass measurement. Do not enter a supported mass in kilograms or pounds directly without converting its applicable weight and resolving the forces at the bearing. Shaft reactions, belt tension, gear forces, acceleration, unbalance, and axial thrust can all affect bearing loads.

Worked Ball Bearing Life Example

For a ball bearing with C = 20,000 N, P = 5,000 N, and n = 1,500 RPM, C/P = 4. Basic L10 is 4³ = 64 million revolutions. L10h is 64,000,000 ÷ 90,000 = approximately 711.11 operating hours. At 8 operating hours per day and 250 operating days per year, this equals approximately 0.356 scheduled years. None of those values establishes a guaranteed service life.

With the same C/P ratio, the roller-bearing exponent gives 410/3, approximately 101.59 million revolutions. That comparison isolates the exponent only. Actual ball and roller bearings have different catalogue capacities, geometries, load factors, stiffness, friction, and application limits. A larger mathematical life alone does not make a bearing a better choice.

Radial Load, Axial Load, and Equivalent Dynamic Load

The basic tab offers four explicit methods. Pure radial uses P = Fr and refuses to ignore a nonzero axial load. Combined factors use P = XFr + YFa. Threshold mode uses P = Fr when Fa/Fr is at or below the supplied threshold e and otherwise uses the supplied combined-load factors. Known P accepts a separately verified equivalent dynamic load. The threshold implementation is suitable only when that exact branch rule matches the catalogue for your bearing and installation.

Fr is radial bearing load, Fa is axial or thrust load, and X and Y are catalogue factors. There is no universal X, Y, or e for all deep-groove ball bearings, angular-contact bearings, tapered roller bearings, cylindrical roller bearings, or spherical roller bearings. Factors may depend on contact angle, arrangement, relative axial load, and which ring rotates. Some designs require additional induced-thrust or arrangement calculations. Obtain the correct equation from the bearing manufacturer before using combined-load mode.

For pure axial loading, the combined method can evaluate a supplied valid Y with Fr = 0. The calculator does not infer thrust-bearing coefficients. A nonzero Fa with Y = 0 is rejected in combined mode. The optional dynamic multiplier scales the equivalent dynamic load only; it does not automatically alter the independently supplied static load. Document why a multiplier is appropriate, and avoid counting the same application allowance twice.

Static Load Rating C₀ and Static Safety Factor s₀

s₀ = C₀ / P₀

C₀ is the basic static load rating, while P₀ is the equivalent static load. They are separate from dynamic C and P. Static checks concern contact deformation under stationary, slowly moving, peak, or shock loads; fatigue hours do not replace them. Enter a verified P₀ directly, or use catalogue-supported static factors X₀ and Y₀ for the implemented linear combination. Some catalogue static equations require a maximum-of-branches rule: calculate that outside the tool and enter the resulting P₀ directly.

The selected minimum static factor is an application input, not a universal recommendation. Required values depend on bearing type, shocks, running accuracy, noise, operating conditions, and manufacturer guidance. The result says whether the calculated ratio meets your entered criterion; it does not certify the assembly as safe. Inverse sizing also shows P₀ = C₀/s₀ as the load corresponding to your selected factor.

Modified Bearing Rating Life and Reliability

Lnm = a₁ × aISO × (C / P)p million revolutions
Lnmh = a₁ × aISO × L10h

The reliability factor a₁ adjusts rating life for a selected reliability level above the basic 90% reference. The life modification factor aISO represents further conditions within its applicable method. This tool keeps the factors separate so that a change in reliability is not confused with a change in lubrication or contamination. With 99% reliability, a₁ = 0.25 in the ISO 281:2007 table used here. If aISO = 1, the worked ball-bearing example becomes 16 million revolutions, approximately 177.78 hours.

Why There Is No Automatic “Clean Oil” Life Multiplier

A lubrication description alone cannot establish aISO. Actual calculations can involve lubricant viscosity at operating temperature, required reference viscosity, contamination factor, fatigue load limit, bearing size, and load. Enter a factor established by the manufacturer's applicable method and identify its source. A default value of 1 is an explicit scenario assumption, not proof that the actual installation has that value.

The optional helpers show viscosity ratio κ = ν/ν₁ and the ratio ηcCu/P, where ν is actual operating viscosity, ν₁ is reference viscosity, ηc is a contamination factor, and Cu is a fatigue load limit. Viscosity in mm²/s is numerically equivalent to centistokes. These helpers do not convert those ratios into aISO: that requires the appropriate equations, charts, limits, and catalogue data. Oil viscosity at a reference temperature is not automatically the viscosity at the bearing's operating temperature.

Manufacturer factors such as SKF aSKF and proprietary NSK life methods have their own definitions and applicability. They should not be relabeled as interchangeable ISO factors without checking the source. Water ingress, electrical erosion, corrosion, severe debris, poor alignment, or unsuitable lubrication can invalidate the assumptions behind a fatigue calculation.

Variable Load and Variable Speed Bearing Life

The duty-cycle calculator accepts up to 100 rows containing equivalent dynamic load P, RPM, duration in hours, and an optional aISO. Use commas, semicolons, or tabs as separators. Loads follow the selected force unit. Each row must describe a segment of a repeating cycle, with a positive duration. Enter equivalent bearing loads rather than unresolved machine forces.

Nᵢ = 60 × nᵢ × tᵢ
P equivalent = [Σ(Nᵢ × Pᵢp) / ΣNᵢ]1/p
Dᵢ = (Nᵢ / 1,000,000) × (Pᵢ / C)p
Basic elapsed life = cycle hours / ΣDᵢ

Equivalent load is weighted by revolutions, not simply by time. A high-load segment at a different speed contributes differently from an equal-duration segment at another speed. Averaging the loads arithmetically and applying the life exponent afterward can substantially overestimate life. The segment table shows the basic fatigue allocation share to make the dominant operating condition visible.

The modified scenario divides each basic segment allocation by its supplied a₁aISO before summing. This is an explicitly stated linear allocation model. The shares are calculated fractions, not measurements of actual fatigue consumed. Changing C, P, or speed can change the physically appropriate modification factor, even though an inverse scenario holds entered factors constant.

Zero-RPM dwell adds elapsed cycle time without adding rolling revolutions. The output therefore separates elapsed duty life from rolling-time life. Static indentation, fretting, false brinelling, and damage during dwell remain outside the model. Zero-load rotation contributes no modeled fatigue allocation, but minimum-load and skidding requirements still apply. A cycle with no rotation or no modeled fatigue allocation is rejected rather than presented as unlimited service life.

Required Dynamic Load Rating and Reverse Sizing

C required = P × [60 × n × target hours / (1,000,000 × a₁ × aISO)]1/p

Inverse sizing answers “What dynamic load rating do I need for a target bearing life?” It also solves for equivalent load and RPM when the other quantities remain fixed. For the ball-bearing example at 5,000 N, 1,500 RPM, and a basic target of 20,000 hours, the required ratio is 1,8001/3, giving C of approximately 60.82 kN.

The resulting load is a fatigue-equation limit for that scenario, not an allowable structural load. The resulting RPM is not a limiting speed, reference speed, thermal speed, or safe operating speed. Check manufacturer speed limits, lubrication, heat generation, seals, cage behavior, and assembly requirements independently. For modified sizing, recalculate the life factor if the changed load or speed changes the physical conditions.

Load Sensitivity and Comparing Two Bearings

The normalized load-versus-life chart illustrates the power law. At unchanged C and speed, raising ball-bearing P by 10% changes life by 1/1.1³, approximately 0.751: about 25% less calculated fatigue life. Reducing P by 20% changes life by 1/0.8³, approximately 1.953. Roller life is more sensitive to a proportional change in load within this equation because its exponent, 10/3, is greater than 3.

The comparison tab permits separate X and Y factors and independent static loads for each bearing. Shared machine loads do not imply shared catalogue coefficients. Compare calculated hours, capacities, and your entered criteria, then verify bore, outside diameter, width, arrangement, clearance, fits, preload, stiffness, temperature, sealing, and installation constraints. The calculator intentionally avoids fabricated part numbers or universal bearing presets.

Optional Weibull and System Survival Illustration

The modified tab includes an optional illustrative Weibull model with a user-selected shape β. It is anchored to the unmodified L10h so that S(L10h) = 0.90. It shows S(t) = exp[ln(0.9) × (t/L10h)β]. This optional model is distinct from the tabulated ISO reliability factors and is not fitted to the ISO table or to field failure data.

For an illustrative series system of N identical independent bearings, survival is S(t)N. Two such bearings at their individual L10h give 0.9² = 0.81 system survival under those assumptions. Shared contamination, common lubrication failures, unequal loads, and dependent failure mechanisms can violate independence. Use an appropriate reliability analysis for a real machine rather than treating this picture as a certified prediction.

Common Input Errors and Engineering Limits

  • Mixing C in kilonewtons with P in newtons changes the ratio by 1,000. Use the unit selector consistently.
  • Using C₀ as C, or using dynamic P as static P₀ without checking the catalogue equation, confuses distinct assessments.
  • Ignoring axial load, induced thrust, peak loading, or load sharing can make the equivalent load unsuitable.
  • At zero RPM the constant-speed hours conversion is undefined. Stationary loading needs a different assessment.
  • Very heavy loads and very light loads can fall outside the ordinary rating model's useful application range. Warnings are prompts for manufacturer review, not automatic acceptance limits.
  • A large fatigue-life value does not account for wear, contamination damage, lubricant aging, corrosion, electrical current, misalignment, installation damage, or seizure.
  • Calculated fatigue life is not remaining useful life. Condition monitoring, inspection, operating history, and failure analysis provide different information.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is L10 bearing life?

L10 is the basic rolling-contact fatigue rating life that 90% of an apparently identical bearing population is expected to reach or exceed under the rating assumptions. It is not guaranteed individual service life.

How do I calculate bearing life in hours?

For positive RPM, L10h = 1,000,000 × (C/P)^p ÷ (60 × RPM), where p is 3 for ball bearings and 10/3 for roller bearings. C and P must use the same force unit.

What is the difference between C and C₀?

C is the basic dynamic load rating used for rolling-fatigue life. C₀ is the basic static load rating used with equivalent static load P₀ for the separate static factor C₀/P₀.

Can I use radial and axial loads together?

Yes, when the supplied equivalent-load equation and catalogue X, Y, and threshold e are appropriate for your bearing. The tool does not determine universal factors or induced thrust.

What reliability factor is used at 99%?

The ISO 281:2007 table used here gives a₁ = 0.25 at 99% reliability. Older or proprietary methods can use different values, so do not mix tables silently.

Does clean lubrication automatically increase life?

No. A supported life modification factor requires the applicable manufacturer method and operating data. Viscosity and contamination helper ratios do not independently establish aISO.

Can I calculate life at zero RPM?

The tool can show a mathematical revolution rating at zero RPM, but hours are undefined. Stationary loads and dwell damage require independent static and application assessments.

How are variable loads and speeds combined?

The duty-cycle model weights load powers by revolutions and sums segment fatigue allocations. It reports both elapsed cycle life and rolling-time life, with a separately stated modified scenario.

Can I find the required bearing dynamic rating?

Yes. Inverse sizing solves for required C, equivalent load, or RPM for a target life while holding the other inputs and entered life factors fixed. These are fatigue-equation results, not mechanical limits.

Does printing include the article?

No. Printing and Save PDF use a separate report containing calculation data, diagrams, and assumptions. CSV and TXT also export report information only.

Does a longer calculated life prove a bearing is suitable?

No. Static capacity, minimum load, speed, fits, clearance, lubrication, temperature, alignment, sealing, and non-fatigue failure mechanisms still need application-specific checks.

Is the Weibull chart an ISO reliability prediction?

No. It is an optional illustrative model anchored to 90% survival at basic L10h using your selected shape factor. The ISO reliability factors remain a separate tabulated method.
