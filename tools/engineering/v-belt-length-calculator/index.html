What this free online V-belt length calculator does

A V-belt length calculator connects three basic drive measurements: the two pulley reference diameters, the distance between shaft centers and the belt’s reference-line circumference. This tool calculates a two-pulley open belt drive, showing both exact geometric length and the common engineering approximation. It also works backwards to find center distance or a missing driven pulley diameter.

Use it for preliminary planning of a motor-to-machine belt drive, checking a proposed pulley change, comparing available belt lengths or discussing a replacement with a supplier. The same circle-and-tangent geometry can describe other two-pulley belts when appropriate reference diameters are known. However, geometry alone does not choose a V-belt section, establish a timing belt tooth count, account for idlers or determine load capacity.

“Exact” refers to an ideal, taut reference line around circular pulleys, not an exact manufactured belt size or a guaranteed installed fit. Real belt construction, reference conventions, tolerances and the installation procedure still matter.

How to calculate V-belt length from pulley diameters

  1. Choose matching units. Use millimeters or inches for all dimensions. Switching units converts current numeric values instead of simply changing their labels.
  2. Choose the calculation mode. Belt-length mode needs both pulley diameters and shaft spacing. The reverse modes need a known belt reference length or a target driven RPM.
  3. Identify the driver. The driver is the pulley on the input shaft. It can be smaller or larger than the driven pulley. Keeping that distinction is essential for speed calculations.
  4. Confirm the diameter and length convention. Use pitch, datum or effective values from compatible specifications. The reference selector does not calculate a conversion between those systems.
  5. Enter center distance. Measure between shaft axes, not between pulley rims. The diagram shows the relationship between the center line, reference circles and tangent belt spans.
  6. Review the results. Check the exact length, approximate length, both wrap angles, pulley ratio and any optional speed calculations.
  7. Compare actual catalog lengths. Paste suitable lengths from a manufacturer’s product list and check the center distance each would require. Confirm that the mounting arrangement leaves appropriate installation and take-up travel.

A blank required measurement or impossible geometry stops the calculation and disables result exports. Optional checks show their own error messages without concealing valid main-drive results. A blank RPM skips speed calculations; zero RPM describes a stopped drive and cannot be used to calculate torque from power.

Pitch length, datum length, effective length and outside length

The visible outside of a belt and pulley is not necessarily the reference used to specify a drive. Pitch length describes a pitch-line convention. Datum length uses a defined datum system associated with pulley geometry. Effective length uses an effective measurement convention. Inside length and outside circumference describe different belt paths again.

Do not assume these names are interchangeable across product families. Autodesk’s V-belt geometry documentation explains that its diameter and length system depends on the belt type and associated reference dimensions. This calculator therefore lets you label the chosen reference but keeps the geometric calculation separate from product-specific conversions.

A ruler across a sheave rim gives outside diameter, not automatically the required reference diameter. Likewise, a belt’s printed number may encode an inside, datum, effective, nominal or other catalog length. Check the product specification, cross section and stated measuring convention before entering a value. If a catalog provides a conversion, use that manufacturer’s definition rather than a universal offset.

Classical V-belt names such as A, B and C, narrow-belt names such as SPZ, SPA, SPB and SPC, and inch-series names such as 3V, 5V and 8V identify families with different dimensions and conventions. This page does not claim that similarly named belts are interchangeable. A numerical length match does not establish groove compatibility.

Open belt length formula: approximation and exact geometry

Let D be the larger reference diameter, d the smaller reference diameter and C the distance between shaft centers. Use matching length units throughout. Define Δ = D − d, α = asin(Δ / (2C)) in radians, and s = √(C² − (Δ/2)²) as the length of each straight tangent span.

Exact reference-line length:
L = 2s + (π/2)(D + d) + Δα

Engineering approximation:
L ≈ 2C + (π/2)(D + d) + (D − d)² / (4C)

Equal pulley shortcut, D = d:
L = 2C + πd

The first expression adds the two straight spans and the two pulley-contact arcs. Unequal pulleys shift the tangent points, so each pulley does not receive a simple half-circle of contact. The approximation replaces that geometry with a convenient correction term. It uses π/2 rather than the rounded coefficient 1.57.

The approximation is generally closer when the diameter difference is small relative to spacing. As the diameters become more unequal and their centers move closer, its error increases. This tool displays the numerical difference for the actual entered drive so you do not have to assume a fixed accuracy percentage.

All valid drives on this page must have C > (D + d)/2: their reference circles must be separated. That also ensures valid external tangents. Actual sheave rims and belt sidewalls can extend beyond those reference circles, so the reported reference-circle gap is not a certified physical clearance.

Worked metric example: 100 mm and 250 mm pulleys

For a 100 mm driver, a 250 mm driven pulley and 500 mm center distance, the diameter difference is 150 mm. The tangent offset angle is asin(150 / 1,000), approximately 8.6269°. Each straight belt span is approximately 494.3430 mm.

The exact reference-line belt length is 1,561.04995 mm. The engineering approximation using π/2 is 1,561.02871 mm, approximately 0.02124 mm shorter in this example. Using 1.57 instead of π/2 gives a different rounded estimate of 1,560.75 mm. These differences do not justify a blanket elongation allowance.

The smaller pulley has about 162.746° of wrap, and the larger has about 197.254°. With the 100 mm pulley driving at 1,450 RPM, the ideal driven speed is 1,450 × 100 / 250 = 580 RPM. The reference-line belt speed is approximately 7.5922 m/s.

Worked imperial example

For 4 in and 10 in pulleys at 20 in shaft spacing, exact geometric belt length is approximately 62.4420 in, and the approximate result is 62.4411 in. The diameter and spacing proportions match the metric example, so the wrap angles and speed ratios are the same. Length units change the size of a drive, not its dimensionless ratio.

Belt center distance calculator: working backwards from length

When you already have a belt reference length and two compatible pulley diameters, select “Center distance from belt length.” The calculator solves the exact length equation numerically on the branch where the reference circles remain separated. If the belt is too short for that geometry, it reports no valid solution instead of inventing a shaft spacing.

For a hand estimate, define Q = L − (π/2)(D + d). Rearranging the approximate equation gives C ≈ [Q + √(Q² − 2(D − d)²)] / 4, using the larger-center solution. This approximation still needs geometric checks and can differ from the exact reverse result.

Changing center distance changes belt path length. The displayed sensitivity is dL/dC = 2√[1 − ((D − d)/(2C))²]. For equal pulleys, that is exactly two: moving the shaft spacing by one millimeter changes the geometric path by two millimeters. For unequal pulleys it is slightly less than two. This is a geometry relationship, not a tension-setting rule.

Find a driven pulley diameter from belt length or target RPM

Use the driven-diameter mode when the driver diameter, center distance and belt reference length are known. The tool searches for the missing positive diameter while requiring the circles to remain separated. Some combinations have no solution: the requested belt may be shorter than any positive second pulley can fit, or longer than a separated second pulley permits at that spacing.

For a target speed, the ideal relationship is n₂ = n₁d₁/d₂, where n₁ is driver RPM, n₂ is driven RPM, d₁ is driver diameter and d₂ is driven diameter. Rearranging gives d₂ = d₁n₁/n₂. In this tool, a user-entered total speed loss k modifies the relationship to n₂ = (n₁d₁/d₂)(1 − k/100).

That loss is an assumption you supply, not a prediction of actual slip or belt creep. It does not change geometric belt length. An available pulley will also have manufacturing dimensions and rating limits, so a calculated decimal diameter is a design target rather than an instruction to machine or buy that exact size.

Pulley wrap angle: contact is not a capacity certificate

On an open drive, the small-pulley contact angle is 180° − 2α and the large-pulley angle is 180° + 2α, with α converted to degrees. Equal pulleys have 180° of wrap each. Increasing shaft spacing or reducing the diameter difference moves the small-pulley wrap closer to 180°.

The optional wrap screen starts at 120°, but that is a screening input rather than a universal permissible minimum. The actual belt product, section, loading and manufacturer contact-angle correction procedure determine whether the drive is adequate. A result above the entered threshold is not proof that it will transmit the requested power without slipping.

For unequal pulleys and a threshold below 180°, the center distance required by that wrap angle alone is C = (D − d) / [2 sin((180° − θ)/2)]. It must still satisfy reference-circle separation and actual hardware-clearance requirements. No finite spacing produces exactly 180° small-pulley wrap with unequal diameters.

Standard V-belt lengths, belt sizing and mounting adjustment

The nearest numerical belt length is not necessarily the best replacement. First identify the correct product family, cross section, groove compatibility and length reference. Then collect the available lengths from that product’s catalog. The candidate table solves the center distance required by each supplied length and compares it with your entered mounting range.

For example, a reference length close to 1,561 mm may require a slightly different shaft spacing than the original 500 mm. A longer belt generally needs a larger operating center distance, while a shorter belt needs a smaller one. Use the calculated center shift to judge whether a candidate is worth checking with the supplier.

Do not add a universal 2%, 3% or 5% to belt length for “stretch.” Manufacturer installation and take-up requirements are specified separately and depend on the belt system. Gates’ maintenance guidance includes allowance tables and an adjustment procedure. Use the applicable product instructions when selecting the belt and mounting travel.

A candidate marked “Within travel” means only that its computed operating spacing lies between your two limits. It does not verify that enough inward movement remains for installation or enough outward movement remains for the specified adjustment. The tool deliberately does not invent those allowances from an arbitrary percentage.

Belt speed, circuit frequency, torque and effective force

Reference-line belt speed is v = πd₁n₁ / 60 when d₁ is in meters and n₁ is in RPM. The calculator also reports feet per minute and the circuit frequency f = v / L, with L in meters. Circuit frequency is how often one point on the belt completes a loop; it is not a measured vibration or a diagnostic resonance frequency.

If transmitted input power P is provided, ideal input shaft torque is T₁ = P/ω₁, where P is in watts and ω₁ = 2πn₁/60. Entered efficiency gives output power P₂ = ηP₁; the output torque estimate uses that output power and the assumed driven speed.

The whole drive’s effective tangential belt force is F = P₁/v. This is the difference between tight-side and slack-side forces, not either individual force and not the tension to apply during installation. It also does not give shaft bearing load or force per belt in a multiple-belt drive. Those require additional design information.

Speed ratio, efficiency and assumed speed loss play different roles. Diameter ratio determines ideal speed change. Entered speed loss modifies driven RPM. Entered efficiency modifies transmitted output power. None of these replaces product ratings, service factors or a complete drive-selection procedure.

Measurement uncertainty and why rounding matters

The tolerance check evaluates the lowest and highest geometric belt lengths for independent ± diameter and center-distance uncertainties. For valid separated reference circles, length increases with either diameter and with center distance. This lets the tool bracket the range at the low and high measurement limits.

If an uncertainty range includes a nonpositive diameter or overlapping circles, the tool asks you to correct it instead of presenting a misleading interval. A geometric interval is not a belt manufacturing-tolerance table or a simulation of elongation under load. It can help explain why measuring a pulley approximately produces a less precise result than the output’s decimal places suggest.

Calculations retain floating-point precision internally, while the display rounds to the selected decimal places. Unit changes preserve significantly more precision than a one-decimal conversion. However, very small differences below the input measurements’ accuracy should not be treated as meaningful purchasing tolerances.

Common V-belt calculation mistakes

  • Confusing diameter and radius. The inputs require full pulley reference diameters.
  • Measuring rim-to-rim spacing. Center distance means shaft axis to shaft axis; the rim gap is a different measurement.
  • Using a mixed length convention. An outside belt circumference cannot simply be compared with a pitch-line result.
  • Assuming the small pulley is always the driver. A larger driver can increase output speed. The input labels must reflect the actual power direction.
  • Adding a generic stretch percentage. Belt selection and installation adjustment need product-specific instructions.
  • Ignoring tensioners or extra pulleys. An idler changes the belt path, contact geometry and required length.
  • Reading a warning as a diagnosis. Low wrap is one design consideration. It does not prove the cause of squeal, heat, wear or vibration.
  • Confusing a geometric result with drive approval. Belt section, belt count, pulley strength, loading, alignment, guarding and allowable speeds remain separate checks.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for V-belt length?

The common open-drive estimate is L ≈ 2C + (π/2)(D + d) + (D − d)²/(4C). This tool also calculates the exact reference-circle tangent-and-arc length described above.

How do I calculate belt length with equal pulleys?

Use L = 2C + πd, where d is either reference diameter and C is shaft center distance. Both pulleys have 180° reference-line wrap.

Can this calculate pulley center distance from a belt length?

Yes. Select center-distance mode and provide both reference diameters and a matching belt reference length. A valid result requires separated reference circles.

Can I find pulley size for a required RPM?

Yes. Target-RPM mode solves the driven reference diameter from the driver diameter, driver speed and your assumed total speed loss. Check the resulting diameter against available, compatible pulleys.

Should I enter pitch diameter or outside diameter?

Enter the reference diameter specified for the chosen length system. A sheave’s measured outside diameter is not automatically its pitch or datum diameter. Use product specifications.

Does choosing datum or effective length convert my dimensions?

No. It changes the reference label only. Both input diameters and any catalog lengths must already use the same product-appropriate system.

Should I add 3% to the calculated V-belt length?

No universal 3% addition is applied. Select an appropriate manufacturer-listed length and provide the specified installation and take-up adjustment for that belt system.

Why does a belt code not match the calculated number?

The marking may use a different length convention, product-specific nominal size or belt family. Identify the full manufacturer specification rather than interpreting all belt numbers as pitch length.

Can this calculator handle a crossed belt drive?

No. It models a two-pulley open drive with same-direction rotation. Crossed drives use different tangents and contact geometry, and conventional V-belt routing must follow the manufacturer’s permitted arrangement.

Does it include an idler, tensioner or three-pulley layout?

No. Additional pulleys change the path. Use a layout-based calculation or suitable drive-design software for those systems.

Does belt length determine output RPM?

For fixed compatible pulley diameters, ideal RPM ratio depends on those diameters, not belt length. Belt length determines feasible spacing, which can affect wrap and installation geometry.

Can the calculated force be used as installation tension?

No. Effective force is the tight-side minus slack-side force required by the entered transmitted power. Installation tension needs the product-specific method and appropriate measurements.

Can I use the result to select a timing belt tooth count?

A timing belt requires its pitch, tooth count, pulley geometry and installation constraints. This V-belt tool does not perform that discrete selection.

Methodology and references

The model assumes two circular pulleys, parallel shafts, an open belt path, straight tangent spans and one consistent diameter/length reference system. Reverse calculations use a bounded numerical search over physically separated reference circles. They do not model belt stiffness, sag, temperature, seating, groove wear or loaded deformation.
