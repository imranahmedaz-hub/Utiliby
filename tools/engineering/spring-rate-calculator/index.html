Spring rate explained: a practical guide to stiffness, force and deflection

A spring rate, spring constant or spring stiffness describes how quickly load changes as a spring moves. For an ideal linear compression spring, a rate of 5 N/mm means each additional millimetre of compression adds 5 N of force. A torsion spring instead resists rotation: its rate is torque per angle, such as N·mm/degree or lbf·in/degree. These quantities have different dimensions and should never be compared as if they were interchangeable.

This free online spring rate calculator supports preliminary analysis of cylindrical helical springs made from round wire. Use it to estimate a replacement coil spring, compare wire sizes, investigate active coil count, plan a bench test or understand a drawing. The result is a model estimate rather than a manufacturer’s load rating. Conical springs, variable-pitch springs, disc springs, leaf springs, flat spiral springs, elastomer springs and suspension assemblies with changing geometry require other models.

How to use the spring rate calculator

  1. Select the physical spring type. Compression springs push, extension springs pull, and torsion springs apply torque through rotating legs. Select the unit system before entering dimensions; switching units later converts the existing numerical values.
  2. Identify the material properties. Use shear modulus G for compression or extension and Young’s modulus E for torsion. Presets are approximate starting points. Replace them with values for the actual grade, temperature and condition when available.
  3. Measure the wire and coil diameter. Use a micrometer for wire thickness where possible. Select whether the coil dimension is mean, outside or inside diameter. Measurements should describe the unloaded spring body.
  4. Count effective coils and select the ends. Inactive compression end turns do not contribute the same deflection as active body turns. Total-to-active conversion is an estimate tied to the chosen end arrangement.
  5. Set a working load or displacement. Enter compression or extension travel in length units, or torsion rotation in degrees. For extension springs, include initial tension if known. For compression springs, enter free length and preferably a measured solid height.
  6. Review the results and limitations together. The chart shows the linear response and selected working point. The warnings identify missing strength limits, coil contact and modeling limitations. Print or save the report for a design discussion.

The diagrams help distinguish wire diameter, coil diameter, axial travel and angular motion. They intentionally simplify the geometry: visible turn count and rotation are capped to keep the picture readable, and the extension hooks are illustrative. Use a dimensioned drawing and a physical spring sample for fit and manufacturing decisions.

Compression spring rate formula

For a close-coiled, round-wire helical spring under an axial load, the basic stiffness equation is:

k = Gd⁴ / (8D³Nₐ)
F = kx
x = F/k

Here k is spring rate, G is shear modulus, d is wire diameter, D is mean coil diameter, Nₐ is active coil count, F is axial force and x is deflection from the unloaded reference. When G is in MPa (N/mm²) and dimensions are in millimetres, k is in N/mm. The calculator uses this consistent internal metric basis and converts the displayed results into imperial units when requested.

The formula assumes a cylindrical spring with an approximately linear elastic response, modest helix angle and no changing coil contact in the modeled travel. It omits detailed end effects and manufacturing residual stresses. A compression spring with progressive pitch can lose active coils as it closes, increasing its rate. In that case, one constant k cannot represent its entire stroke.

Worked compression spring example

Consider music-wire-like material with G = 79,300 MPa, wire diameter 2 mm, mean diameter 20 mm and 8 active coils. The estimated rate is 2.478125 N/mm. At 10 mm compression, the predicted force is about 24.7813 N and the ideal stored energy is about 0.123906 J. With closed and ground ends, 10 estimated total coils give a nominal solid height of 20 mm. A 50 mm free length therefore leaves 30 mm of geometric travel to coil contact; choosing a 10% travel reserve leaves 27 mm of selected travel.

Those clearance numbers are not a strength rating. The same working point needs wire stress, fatigue, buckling and installation review. A spring that fits geometrically may still be unsuitable for its load or cycle life.

Extension spring rate and initial tension

An extension spring’s body rate follows the same axial geometry equation as a compression spring. Initial tension adds a force offset: a close-wound extension spring typically requires an opening force before its body coils begin separating. In the ideal opening region:

F = Fᵢ + kx
x = (F − Fᵢ)/k, for F ≥ Fᵢ

Fᵢ is initial tension and x is incremental extension after the body starts opening. If the requested applied force is below initial tension, the tool predicts zero body opening and does not report the open-coil stress estimate. It does not model small elastic movement of hooks or closed coils before opening. At zero opening in displacement mode, the displayed load is the ideal opening threshold, not a claim that an unloaded spring has an external force acting on it.

For the example body rate of 2.478125 N/mm and initial tension of 5 N, an extension of 10 mm requires approximately 29.7813 N. Dividing that total force by 10 mm gives 2.97813 N/mm, which is not the body spring rate because it includes the initial-tension offset. Use the slope between two opened-coil load points to measure the rate instead.

Hooks and loops often govern extension spring performance. They experience their own bending and torsional stresses, and their compliance can change the measured assembly stiffness. This calculator does not establish maximum extension or hook capacity. Body shear stress alone cannot certify an extension spring design.

Torsion spring rate: torque, angle and bending

A helical torsion spring applies torque, but its wire primarily bends. Its stiffness therefore depends on Young’s modulus E rather than the axial spring model’s shear modulus G. This calculator uses an ideal elastic bending formulation:

Nₑ = Nbody + (L₁ + L₂)/(3πD)
kθ = Ed⁴/(64DNₑ), in N·mm/radian
kdeg = kθ × π/180
T = kdeg × θdeg

Nₑ is the effective turn count. The leg term is a simple approximation for straight cantilever legs loaded by perpendicular forces; enter zero leg lengths to calculate the body-only rate. Bent legs, different load directions, contact constraints, short lever arms and double torsion spring geometry require a more specific treatment. Manufacturer methods may use different practical constants or corrections, so identify the modeling basis when comparing calculations.

Torque equals force multiplied by its perpendicular moment arm: T = Fr. A 100 N·mm torque acting at a 25 mm perpendicular arm corresponds to 4 N of force. The perpendicular arm is the distance from the axis to the force’s line of action; it need not equal the physical leg length. This distinction is essential when a force acts at an angle.

Worked torsion spring example

With E = 206,000 MPa, d = 2 mm, D = 20 mm and 8 body coils, excluding leg compliance, the ideal rate is 321.875 N·mm/radian, or about 5.61778 N·mm/degree. At 30° rotation, the predicted torque is approximately 168.533 N·mm. Adding flexible straight legs lowers the calculated angular stiffness because their bending contributes to the total rotation.

Real torsion springs also change coil diameter and body length during winding. Installation must account for shaft clearance, winding direction, friction between turns, leg motion, end stops and load direction. The displayed animation does not simulate those dimensional changes. Loading in the intended winding direction and checking the complete loaded geometry are design tasks, not conclusions supplied by the tool.

Using outside diameter directly in a formula that requires mean diameter can create a substantial rate error because D is cubed in the axial model. For 2 mm wire and 22 mm outside diameter, the correct mean diameter is 20 mm. An inside diameter of 18 mm describes the same body geometry.

A spring index around 4–12 is a common preliminary manufacturing range, not a universal acceptance standard. Tight coils can be difficult to form and show stronger curvature effects. Large index coils raise other geometry and handling concerns. Practical limits depend on material, wire size, manufacturing process and the application. The calculator reports unusual index values for review rather than labeling a spring safe or unsafe.

Solid height, coil bind and working travel

Compression spring solid height is the approximate length at coil contact. A single total-coils-times-wire formula does not cover all end styles. The tool uses these nominal estimates, with a measured solid-height override when available:

Compression endsEstimated total turnsEstimated solid height
Plain, not groundNₜ = Nₐd(Nₜ + 1)
Plain and groundNₜ = Nₐ + 1dNₜ
Closed, not groundNₜ = Nₐ + 2d(Nₜ + 1)
Closed and groundNₜ = Nₐ + 2dNₜ

Actual solid height varies with end preparation, grinding, wire tolerances and manufacturing details. Geometric travel is free length minus solid height. The selected reserve percentage reduces that travel to leave clearance. It does not derive a permissible operating stroke from material strength or fatigue requirements. Loads computed at or beyond coil contact are explicitly identified as linear extrapolations.

Compression spring buckling depends on free-length slenderness, deflection, end restraint and any guide rod or bore. The ratio L₀/D is useful descriptive information, but one ratio cannot establish a universal critical buckling load. A guide may improve lateral support while also introducing friction, wear and clearance requirements. The tool deliberately reports slenderness without issuing a certified buckling pass.

Wire stress and the Wahl factor

Axial loading twists the wire in a compression or extension spring. The estimated body shear stress uses the Wahl correction for wire curvature and direct shear:

Kᵥ = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C
τ = Kᵥ × 8FD/(πd³)

For a torsion spring’s inner wire surface, the modeled bending stress uses a different correction:

Kᵢ = (4C² − C − 1)/(4C(C − 1))
σ = Kᵢ × 32T/(πd³)

The stresses are different physical quantities. An allowable bending stress should not be substituted for an allowable shear stress. Preset elastic moduli do not define yield strength, ultimate tensile strength or an allowable working stress. Strength depends on grade, wire diameter, processing, heat treatment, surface condition, temperature and duty.

If you supply a suitable allowable wire stress, the calculator reports allowable divided by estimated stress. That ratio is a static comparison using your input, not a fatigue safety factor. Repeated loading needs mean and alternating stress assessment, cycle-life targets, surface and corrosion considerations, manufacturing residual stresses and appropriate design data. For extension springs, end stresses need separate analysis.

Calculate spring constant from two measured loads

A practical load-test estimate uses the slope between two points:

kmeasured = (F₂ − F₁)/(x₂ − x₁)
For torsion: kmeasured = (T₂ − T₁)/(θ₂ − θ₁)

For example, 10 N at 2 mm and 30 N at 6 mm gives 5 N/mm. With those particular points, the extrapolated intercept is zero. A nonzero intercept can indicate preload, extension initial tension or a displacement origin that differs from the unloaded reference. It should not automatically be interpreted as a defect.

Use a calibrated load instrument, stable fixtures and several points across the intended elastic travel. Keep the spring seated consistently, avoid coil contact, and record loading and unloading if hysteresis matters. Two points provide a secant stiffness, not proof that the spring is linear. An extension body test should use points after opening. A torque test should record the load’s actual perpendicular arm and the angular reference.

Target rate, wire tolerance and spring sizing

The target-rate helper reverses the selected model to calculate an alternative wire diameter, mean coil diameter or active coil count. Each row changes only one quantity. Combining all three suggestions would solve a different geometry and would not generally achieve the requested rate. Candidate dimensions are not rounded to standard wire gauges or verified for packaging, stress or manufacturability.

For axial springs, wire diameter has a fourth-power effect, mean coil diameter has an inverse cubic effect, and active coil count has an inverse linear effect. Increasing wire diameter by 5% multiplies rate by 1.05⁴, approximately 1.21551: about 21.6% stiffer. Increasing mean diameter by 5% reduces axial rate to about 86.4% of the original. Doubling active coils halves the ideal rate.

The sensitivity table applies a user-selected wire variation while holding mean diameter fixed. If outside diameter is instead constrained by the manufacturing process, changing wire thickness also changes mean diameter, so the sensitivity must be recalculated with that actual constraint. Wire variation changes stress and clearances as well as stiffness. A production tolerance analysis should include all relevant dimensions and material variation.

Natural frequency and spring energy

For a simple axial spring–mass model, the approximate undamped natural frequency is fₙ = √(kSI/meffective)/(2π), where kSI is N/m. The optional one-third coil mass term approximates the contribution of an ideal distributed spring with one end fixed. The estimated coil mass excludes extension hooks and uses a simplified wire length. Fixture mass, damping, boundary conditions and spring dynamics can change the result.

For torsion, the equivalent model is fₙ = √(kθ,SI/I)/(2π), with rotational stiffness in N·m/radian and attached inertia I in kg·m². This torsion estimate excludes the spring’s own distributed rotational inertia. Neither calculation is a spring-surge analysis. No universal frequency multiplier guarantees freedom from resonance; evaluate excitation frequencies, harmonics, damping and the complete mechanism.

Compression energy is ½kx². Incremental work to open an ideal extension spring through x is Fᵢx + ½kx². Torsion energy is ½kθθ² with θ in radians. The extension value describes work added during opening, not the total manufacturing energy already present in a close-wound spring.

Parallel, series, preload and suspension wheel rate

Two ideal linear springs in parallel have effective rate k₁ + k₂ because they share displacement and their forces add. In series, the rate is k₁k₂/(k₁ + k₂) because they share load and their displacements add. Angular springs follow analogous relationships when their connection satisfies the same conditions. Stops, slack, friction, preload and initial tension can introduce different operating regions.

Preloading a linear compression spring shifts its force at an installed position but does not change its basic constant rate. If the spring is already compressed x₀, moving it an additional x gives force k(x₀ + x). Specify whether your displacement is measured from free length or the installed position. This tool’s main axial displacement is measured from its stated unloaded or opening reference.

For vehicle suspension, coil spring rate differs from wheel rate. If motion ratio is defined as spring displacement divided by wheel displacement, and remains constant, wheel rate is approximately spring rate multiplied by the square of that ratio. Real suspension geometry, installation angle, anti-roll bars, tire stiffness, bump stops and changing motion ratio need a vehicle-specific analysis. The main calculator does not recommend a spring for a vehicle, garage door or other safety-critical mechanism.

Frequently asked questions

What is a free spring rate calculator online?

It is a browser-based way to estimate spring constant from geometry and material or measured load points. This tool calculates axial and angular stiffness and provides a report without requiring an account.

What is the formula for spring rate?

For the basic cylindrical round-wire axial model, k = Gd⁴/(8D³Nₐ). A torsion spring uses a bending model with E and an angular rate. Choose the spring type before applying a formula.

How is spring rate calculated in metric vs imperial units?

Use consistent units. The calculator converts its inputs and displays axial rate in N/mm or lbf/in and torsion rate in N·mm/degree or lbf·in/degree. Modulus and stress are displayed in MPa or ksi.

Why does wire diameter dominate the spring rate?

The ideal stiffness depends on d⁴. Small wire changes can produce substantial rate changes. This comparison assumes the other model dimensions remain fixed.

What is the spring index and why does it matter?

Spring index is mean coil diameter divided by wire diameter. It helps describe coil tightness and curvature effects. A typical preliminary range is not a guarantee of stability or manufacturability.

What is the Wahl factor and corrected shear stress?

The Wahl factor adjusts an axial spring’s nominal wire shear stress for curvature and direct shear. Torsion spring bending uses a separate correction; the two should not be interchanged.

How do I calculate the natural frequency of a spring?

Use a spring–mass or rotational spring–inertia model with consistent SI units. The tool’s frequency is a simplified system estimate. It does not establish spring surge frequency or a universal required frequency ratio.

What is the solid height of a spring?

It is the compression spring length at coil contact. The estimate depends on end geometry; dNₜ applies to the ground-end arrangements used here, while nominal unground ends use d(Nₜ + 1). A measured value is preferable.

When does a compression spring buckle?

Stability depends on length-to-diameter ratio, loading, deflection, end support and guides. No single slenderness threshold certifies a spring’s buckling behavior. Review the complete restraint and operating geometry.

Does this tool also handle extension and torsion springs?

Yes. Extension includes initial tension and estimates body properties, excluding hook capacity. Torsion uses Young’s modulus, angular stiffness and body bending stress, with optional approximate straight-leg compliance.

Can I print the calculation without the article?

Yes. Print / Save PDF builds a dedicated report containing calculation values, diagrams, load samples, helper results and warnings. Navigation, share controls and this explanatory article are excluded. CSV and TXT downloads also contain report data only.

Why does the calculated rate differ from a supplier’s value?

Check mean versus outside diameter, effective coil count, end details, material modulus, load-test reference and model assumptions. Hooks, flexible legs, coil contact and residual stresses can affect the measured response. Supplier values may describe a specific tested load interval.
