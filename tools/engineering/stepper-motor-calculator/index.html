What is a stepper motor?

A stepper motor is a brushless DC motor that divides a full rotation into a fixed number of equal steps. Unlike a conventional DC motor that spins continuously when power is applied, a stepper motor moves in discrete increments each time the driver sends a pulse. A standard hybrid stepper has 200 steps per revolution — a step angle of 1.8° — and holding torque strong enough to stop and hold a load without feedback.

Because the position is determined by the number of pulses sent rather than by any feedback sensor, stepper motors operate in an open-loop system. The controller assumes the motor moved when it was told to move, which is why lost steps — caused by resonance, excessive load or stepping faster than the winding can keep up with — are the main failure mode.

Stepper motors are the default choice for 3D printers, CNC routers, laser cutters, camera sliders, robot arms, packaging machinery and scientific instruments — anywhere precise, repeatable positioning is required without the cost and complexity of a servo system.

Key stepper motor formulas

Maximum speed and minimum time per step

At high step rates the winding becomes inductive and the current cannot rise fast enough to produce full torque. The first-order approximation for the maximum step rate is:

Max Speed (steps/sec) = V / (2 × L × Imax)

Where V is the supply voltage in volts, L is the winding inductance in henries and Imax is the rated current per phase in amps. The minimum time per step is the inverse:

Min Time/Step (ms) = (2 × L × Imax) / V × 1000

When L is given in millihenries (as most datasheets do), remember to divide by 1000 before using it in the formula.

Power dissipation

The maximum power the motor can dissipate is the product of the applied voltage and the rated current:

Pmax = Imax × V

This is a simplified upper bound. Real power consumption depends on the driver's current-chopping behaviour, the duty cycle of the motion profile, and the motor's copper losses (I²R). It is a useful figure for heatsink sizing and driver selection.

Steps per millimetre (CNC and 3D printing)

The number of motor steps required to move the tool or bed by 1 mm depends on the drive mechanism. For a lead screw or ball screw:

Steps/mm = (steps/rev × microstepping) / (lead in mm × gear ratio)

For a belt-and-pulley drive:

Steps/mm = (steps/rev × microstepping) / (belt pitch × pulley teeth × gear ratio)

For rack-and-pinion:

Steps/mm = (steps/rev × microstepping) / (π × pinion pitch diameter × gear ratio)

These are the numbers you enter into GRBL, Mach3, Marlin, Klipper or any other motion controller as steps_per_mm.

Resonance frequency

A stepper motor and its attached load form a spring-mass system. The natural frequency at which the rotor oscillates is:

f_res = (1 / 2π) × √(T_holding × spr / (2π × (J_rotor + J_load)))

Where J is in kg·m² and T is in N·m. When the step rate crosses this frequency the rotor resonates with increasing amplitude, causing noise, vibration and — if sustained — lost steps. Common fixes include microstepping past the resonance, mechanical damping, or avoiding the resonant speed band in the motion profile.

Torque at a given speed

Available torque drops as step frequency rises, following the winding's RL time constant:

T(f) = T₀ / √(1 + (2π f L / R)²)

Where T₀ is the low-speed holding torque, f is the step frequency, L is the inductance and R is the phase resistance. Plotting this curve against your load torque tells you the maximum speed at which the motor can still drive the load without stalling.

Microstepping: resolution vs torque

Microstepping subdivides each full step by driving the two coils with sinusoidal currents of varying amplitude. A 1/16 microstep setting turns a 200-step motor into a 3,200-step motor — a 16× improvement in positional resolution. But microstepping does not increase torque, and in practice it reduces it.

Common stepper motor selection mistakes

  • Selecting on holding torque alone. Holding torque is the torque at zero speed. The torque that matters is at your operating speed — which is often 30 to 70 percent lower. Always check the torque-speed curve.
  • Ignoring the inertia ratio. A load more than 10× the rotor inertia causes ringing, overshoot and lost steps. For smooth, high-performance motion keep it between 1:1 and 5:1.
  • Undersizing the power supply. A stepper needs a supply at least 8× its rated voltage — and higher voltage means higher top speed. Running a 3 V motor on 5 V cripples its performance.
  • Using microstepping for torque. Microstepping improves resolution, not torque. If you need more torque, buy a bigger motor or increase the supply voltage.
  • Running at resonance. A motor driven exactly at its natural frequency will vibrate, lose steps and eventually fail. Use microstepping, half-stepping or acceleration ramps to skip past the resonance band.
  • Forgetting acceleration. Starting a motor at full speed is impossible. Ramp the step rate up gradually — steppers have limited torque, and the acceleration torque required is often much higher than the steady-state torque.
  • Setting the current too high. Rated current is a thermal limit, not a performance target. Overcurrent burns motors without improving torque.

Stepper motor vs servo motor

Steppers and servos are the two main options for precise motion control. Steppers are simpler and cheaper; servos are faster and more accurate under varying load.

FeatureStepperServo
CostLowHigh
FeedbackNone (open loop)Encoder (closed loop)

Position accuracyExcellent at low speedExcellent at all speeds
Lost stepsPossible under overloadAutomatically corrected
Typical use3D printers, light CNCIndustrial motion, robotics

For hobbyist and small-batch applications, steppers remain the dominant choice. Servos take over where high dynamic performance, heavy load variation or closed-loop reliability are required.

How do I calculate the maximum speed of a stepper motor?

Max speed (steps/sec) = V / (2 × L × Imax), where V is supply voltage, L is winding inductance in henries and Imax is rated current per phase.

How do I calculate steps per mm for a CNC machine?

For a lead screw: steps/mm = (steps per rev × microstepping) / lead in mm. For a belt: steps/mm = (steps per rev × microstepping) / (belt pitch × pulley teeth).

What is microstepping and how does it affect torque?

Microstepping subdivides each full step into smaller increments. It improves resolution and smoothness but reduces available torque. A 1/16 setting delivers roughly 99 percent of full-step torque in theory, but real torque is lower.

What is stepper motor resonance?

Resonance occurs when the step rate matches the natural frequency of the motor and load. At that point the rotor oscillates with increasing amplitude, causing vibration, noise and lost steps.

What is the load inertia ratio?

The ratio of load inertia to rotor inertia. For stepper motors, aim for under 10:1, ideally between 1:1 and 5:1 for smooth motion.

What voltage should I use for a stepper motor?

A common rule is 8 to 20 times the motor's rated voltage when using a current-chopping driver. A 3 V motor typically runs well on 24 V or 36 V.
