Free online stepper motor calculator: from datasheet to motion settings

A stepper motor calculator connects three different questions: how many STEP pulses a controller must generate, how far a mechanism moves for those pulses, and whether a motor can supply the required torque during the motion. Answering one question does not answer the others. A mechanically correct steps-per-millimetre setting can still demand too many pulses from a controller or too much acceleration torque from a motor.

This tool supports preliminary checks for common two-phase stepper systems used in CNC routers, 3D printers, laser machines, positioning stages, camera sliders and rotary tables. It calculates motor RPM, full-step and microstep resolution, pulse timing, winding estimates, drive geometry, measured travel calibration, rest-to-rest motion time and load torque. The torque curve tab accepts actual manufacturer or measured data instead of treating a generic curve as a motor specification.

How to use the four calculator modes

  1. Speed & Power: enter full steps per motor revolution, input microsteps per full step, requested RPM and driver timing requirements. Add winding resistance and inductance for electrical screening. Select whether the entered current is sinusoidal peak, per-phase RMS, or a constant current in both phases.
  2. CNC / Steps per mm: choose a lead screw, timing belt, rack and pinion, friction roller or rotary output. Enter the actual travel geometry and motor-to-output reduction. Select output length units, then set feed, acceleration and move distance.
  3. Torque-Speed Curve: enter at least two increasing RPM points from the actual motor/driver curve. Record the conditions in the annotation field. Compare the curve with required torque and your selected margin. The optional illustrative model is educational, not a motor rating.
  4. Motion & Load: use the CNC mechanism and motion settings to estimate the uphill acceleration-stage torque. Enter translating mass, opposing force, transmission efficiency and relevant rotating inertia. Supply running torque at the calculated speed to make a capacity comparison.
  5. Export the relevant calculation: use Print / Save PDF, CSV, TXT or Copy report. The active mode is exported by default. Select “Include all valid calculator sections” when a combined report is useful.

Required fields are validated rather than silently replaced with defaults. Blank optional fields skip the corresponding assessment. The included values are examples, not recommendations for a particular NEMA frame size, driver board or machine. The motor-angle and travel sliders preview commanded positions; they do not read an encoder or verify that a physical motor followed the command.

Stepper RPM, step angle and pulse frequency

Full steps per revolution describe the motor’s native stepping geometry. A 1.8° motor has 200 full steps per revolution; a 0.9° motor has 400. Driver microstepping divides a full step into additional command increments. For a STEP/DIR interface:

Full steps/revolution = 360° / full-step angle
Pulses/revolution = full steps/revolution × input microsteps
STEP frequency = RPM × pulses/revolution / 60
RPM = 60 × STEP frequency / pulses/revolution

For a 200-step motor at 16 microsteps and 300 RPM, the controller needs 16,000 pulses/s, or 16 kHz. Its STEP period is 62.5 µs. The full-step-equivalent rate is 1,000 steps/s. These are command-rate calculations; they do not show that the motor has enough running torque to maintain 300 RPM under load.

Use the microstep subdivision actually commanded at the STEP input. Some drivers internally interpolate lower-resolution input pulses into finer phase-current waveforms. If a driver accepts 16 input microsteps and internally interpolates to 256, entering 256 in the steps/mm formula would overcount the required input pulses. Consult the driver’s interface configuration.

Pulse width limits and controller ceilings

Both the minimum high time and the minimum low time matter. A simple pulse-timing ceiling is 1,000,000 divided by the sum of those times in microseconds. The calculator uses the lower of this ceiling and the optional controller STEP-rate limit. Setup and hold times for direction changes, processing capacity, simultaneous axes, communication, acceleration scheduling and real firmware implementation can impose additional limits.

For 2 µs high and 2 µs low, the timing-only ceiling is 250 kHz. If the controller ceiling is 100 kHz, the effective ceiling is 100 kHz. With 3,200 input pulses per revolution, that corresponds to a command ceiling of 1,875 RPM. It is not a claim that the motor can run at that speed.

Steps per mm for a lead screw or ball screw

A screw’s lead is the axial movement per output revolution. Thread pitch is the spacing between adjacent threads. They are equal for a single-start screw, while a multi-start screw has lead = pitch × number of starts.

Steps/mm = (full steps/revolution × microsteps × reduction ratio) / screw lead in mm

A 200-step motor, 16× input microstepping, 5 mm screw lead and direct coupling give 640 steps/mm. The command increment is 0.0015625 mm per pulse, or 1.5625 µm. The full-step increment is 0.025 mm. Those values describe commanded geometry, not verified repeatability or positioning accuracy.

A screw with 2 mm pitch and four starts has 8 mm lead. With the same motor and microstepping, it gives 400 steps/mm. Entering pitch as lead would produce a fourfold scale error. Use the screw drawing or measured travel per revolution if the designation is unclear.

All linear mechanisms use pulses per output revolution divided by travel per output revolution. For a 2 mm pitch belt and a 20-tooth pulley, travel is 40 mm per revolution. A 200-step motor at 16× gives 80 steps/mm, or a 12.5 µm command increment. At 3,000 mm/min, the controller needs 4 kHz and the motor runs at 75 RPM with direct coupling.

The belt pitch circle, rack pitch diameter and effective roller diameter describe working contact geometry. Outside diameters are generally not interchangeable with these quantities. Filament compression against an extruder hob, belt elasticity, rack backlash, roller slip and loaded deformation can all make measured travel differ from ideal geometry.

Gear reduction direction matters

The ratio is motor revolutions per output revolution. Enter 5 for a 5:1 reduction: the motor makes five revolutions while the screw, pulley or output makes one. The ratio multiplies steps per unit and motor RPM for the same output feed. It also reduces ideal motor-reflected output inertia by the square of the ratio.

For a toothed reduction, calculate the ratio from driven teeth divided by driving teeth. Multiple stages multiply. Use the actual tooth counts when an advertised ratio is rounded. A reduction can improve torque leverage while increasing motor speed and introducing efficiency losses, inertia, backlash and gearbox constraints.

Steps per degree and imperial output

For rotary motion, steps/degree = full steps/revolution × microsteps × reduction / 360. A 200-step motor at 16× with a 5:1 reduction gives about 44.4444 pulses per degree of output rotation. Feed is degrees/minute and acceleration is degrees/s² in this mode. A length-unit selector does not apply to rotary output.

For a linear axis, steps/inch = steps/mm × 25.4. The output-unit switch converts entered feed, acceleration, move distance and calibration travel to preserve the same physical motion. Mechanical pitch, lead and diameter inputs remain in millimetres as their labels specify. Motor electrical and inertia inputs retain their stated SI-based units.

Travel calibration, E-steps and rotation distance

Compare a commanded move with measured movement only after the axis is mechanically sound and not losing steps. The scale correction is:

Corrected steps/unit = old steps/unit × commanded travel / measured travel

If the current value is 80 steps/mm, a 100 mm command that moves 99.5 mm implies approximately 80.40201 steps/mm. Recheck with a suitable measuring instrument and several movements. A reverse-direction discrepancy suggests backlash or lost motion; a load-dependent discrepancy suggests slip, compliance or missed steps. Scaling the firmware to hide those effects can make other moves worse.

For the corresponding effective travel per motor revolution, the correction runs in the opposite direction: new rotation distance = old rotation distance × measured / commanded. The tool reports both directions of correction so an E-steps correction is not accidentally applied to a rotation-distance setting.

Extruders need special care because effective hob diameter can vary with grip, filament and load. Calibrate under an appropriate controlled procedure and avoid compensating for a blocked nozzle or slipping drive with a scale change. For geometrically defined Cartesian axes, begin with hardware-derived travel rather than treating every dimensional printing error as an axis scale problem.

Acceleration distance, triangular moves and motion time

A short move may never reach the requested feed. This calculator models a symmetric rest-to-rest move with constant acceleration, constant-speed cruise when possible and equal deceleration. It excludes jerk limits, corner blending and controller-specific motion planning.

Distance to accelerate to v = v²/(2a)
Rest-to-rest distance needed for cruise = v²/a
Short-move peak velocity = √(distance × acceleration)

At 50 mm/s and 500 mm/s², acceleration needs 2.5 mm, and deceleration needs another 2.5 mm. A 100 mm move has a trapezoidal velocity profile: 0.1 s acceleration, 1.9 s cruise and 0.1 s deceleration, totaling 2.1 s. A 1 mm move instead has a triangular profile, peaks at about 22.3607 mm/s and takes approximately 0.0894427 s.

The integer pulse count rounds a requested move to the nearest command increment and reports the geometric rounding error. This is a discrete-command effect only. It does not represent backlash, full-step angle error, microstep current distortion or measured machine accuracy.

Load torque, inertia and stepper motor sizing

Motor selection needs operating torque at operating speed, including acceleration. The mechanical tab estimates an uphill acceleration stage. Added opposing force can represent drag or process force. For a translating load, gravity adds m g sin(incline), with g = 9.80665 m/s².

Linear steady motor torque = opposing force × travel per output revolution / (2π × reduction × efficiency)
Rotary steady motor torque = opposing output torque / (reduction × efficiency)
Acceleration torque = effective motor-side inertia × motor angular acceleration

Use metres in the linear torque expression to obtain N·m. The calculator makes that conversion internally. Translating mass reflects through the mechanism as m[travel/(2π × reduction)]². Rotating output inertia reflects as Joutput/reduction². Rotor inertia remains at the motor shaft. Transmission efficiency increases load-side torque demand; it does not increase the motor’s own rotor inertia.

The tab adds steady load, acceleration torque and additional motor friction, then multiplies by your selected margin. Compare that result with known running torque at the peak attained motor RPM. The curve-transfer button accepts actual user-supplied curve data within its range and refuses the illustrative sample or proxy. This comparison does not evaluate every instant of a duty cycle.

Inertia ratio is reflected load inertia divided by rotor inertia. A large ratio can complicate acceleration and settling, but there is no universal limit applicable to all open-loop, closed-loop and geared systems. Reported inertia units are g·cm², with 1 g·cm² = 10⁻⁷ kg·m². Include screw, pulley, coupling, gearbox and fixture inertia where relevant.

Holding torque versus running torque

Holding torque describes an energized stationary motor under specified conditions. It is not the torque available across all RPM. Pull-out torque curves describe running capability under particular motor, driver, current, supply, connection and test conditions. Start/stop capability and acceleration can impose additional restrictions.

The curve tab uses piecewise linear interpolation between supplied points and refuses to extrapolate outside them. A positive comparison at one RPM is not a stability, fatigue or thermal certification. Verify that the curve conditions match your system, and consider torque over the whole operating range rather than only at the endpoint.

The optional current-rise proxy is deliberately labeled illustrative. It estimates current built from zero over a full-step interval, caps it at the entered current and scales holding torque by the current fraction. It omits current-vector rotation, magnetic and mechanical behavior and driver regulation details. Use it to discuss parameter sensitivity, not to order a motor or establish maximum feed.

Winding inductance, current rise and electrical power

For an ideal RL winding with initially zero current and constant effective voltage, i(t) = (Veffective/R)[1 − exp(−tR/L)]. The RL time constant is L/R. Effective voltage here is bus voltage minus the optional opposing back-EMF estimate. Driver drops are omitted. If the requested target current is at or above the asymptotic Veffective/R value, it cannot be reached in finite time in this model.

Real stepper drivers commutate, regulate and decay winding current. Their behavior is not equivalent to independently charging an empty winding on every pulse. Supply voltage, phase resistance and inductance alone cannot determine a reliable maximum motor RPM. Use verified torque-speed data, driver timing and load checks.

Copper heat is the sum of winding RMS current squared times resistance. With sinusoidal phase currents having peak I, the ideal total for two equal phases is I²R. With per-phase RMS I, it is 2I²R. Two phases continuously carrying I also give 2I²R. For 1.5 A and 3.3 Ω, these conventions give 7.425 W or 14.85 W respectively.

These values exclude driver loss, iron loss, mechanical output and changing duty. Bus voltage multiplied by a phase-current setting is not a dependable supply-power estimate for a chopping driver. Supply selection also needs measured or modeled bus current, simultaneous motors, duty, efficiency, regeneration, headroom and the driver’s operating voltage limits. Do not use an arbitrary voltage multiplier in place of a driver datasheet.

Microstepping resolution, incremental torque and resonance

Microstepping increases the number of commanded positions and can smooth motion. It does not guarantee equal physical increments or proportionally better absolute accuracy. An ideal incremental-torque comparison is holding torque × sin(90°/microsteps). At 16×, this is about 9.8% of holding torque for the single-increment restoring change, not a statement that total running torque falls to 9.8%.

Friction, detent torque, load and current-control error can prevent the shaft from responding to each individual microstep. Increasing microsteps also increases required STEP frequency at the same RPM. Choose a setting that balances smoothness, controller capacity and actual positioning requirements. NEMA frame numbers specify mounting dimensions; they do not uniquely specify torque, current, inductance or safe speed.

The electrical tab’s natural-frequency estimate assumes a two-phase sinusoidal torque-angle stiffness with effective rotor tooth count equal to full steps/revolution divided by four. It is a small-angle, undamped estimate, not a measured resonance band. Excitation, damping, load, driver behavior and system compliance affect oscillation and lost-step behavior. No displayed frequency or fixed frequency ratio establishes resonance-free motion.

Common mistakes and practical checks

  • Entering multi-start thread pitch where the calculation requires screw lead.
  • Reversing reduction ratio or counting it twice in firmware rotation distance.
  • Using rack outside diameter instead of pitch diameter.
  • Using driver interpolation instead of actual input microsteps.
  • Comparing acceleration demand with holding torque rather than running torque.
  • Confusing peak and RMS phase current when estimating copper heat.
  • Assuming a small command increment proves machine accuracy.
  • Correcting steps/mm to mask backlash, belt slip or lost steps.
  • Ignoring direction timing, controller limits, driver temperature or regenerated voltage.

Frequently asked questions

What is a free stepper motor calculator online?

It calculates step commands, mechanism travel, calibration, motion timing and preliminary torque demand in a browser. Electrical screening and illustrative models are distinguished from actual motor ratings.

How do I calculate the maximum speed of a stepper motor?

There is no dependable maximum-RPM calculation from supply voltage and winding inductance alone. Check driver pulse limits, the actual torque-speed curve, acceleration, load and thermal conditions. The displayed command ceiling is a signal limit, not a certified motor speed.

How do I calculate steps per mm for a CNC machine or 3D printer?

Multiply full steps per motor revolution by input microsteps and motor-to-output reduction, then divide by travel per output revolution. Screw travel is lead; belt travel is pitch times pulley teeth.

What is microstepping and how does it affect torque?

Microstepping subdivides command positions and can improve smoothness. The ideal incremental restoring torque is proportional to sin(90 degrees divided by the microstep count). It does not mean total running torque falls by that fraction, and command resolution does not guarantee accuracy.

What is stepper motor resonance and why does it matter?

The rotor and load can oscillate under excitation. The tool reports an ideal small-angle natural-frequency estimate when suitable inputs are present, without certifying an operating resonance band or stability.

What is the load inertia ratio and what value should it be?

It is motor-reflected load inertia divided by rotor inertia. Acceptable ratios depend on the actual motor, driver, gearing, acceleration and required settling. The calculator does not apply a universal pass/fail threshold.

What voltage should I use for a stepper motor?

Use a supply within the driver’s documented operating limits and a verified phase-current setting suitable for the motor. Consider the specified torque-speed conditions, thermal performance and regeneration. Do not select voltage from an arbitrary multiplier alone.

What does the minimum time per step mean?

STEP pulse high/low timing is an interface constraint. RL current-rise time is a separate electrical estimate. Neither should be treated as a universal motor step interval or stall boundary.

Yes. Calculator use is free and does not require an account. Calculations run in the browser; shared links encode the inputs you choose to share.

Does this calculator handle both metric and imperial units?

Linear output supports steps/mm and steps/inch with physical feed and travel conversion. Rotary output uses steps/degree. Geometry stays in labelled millimetres and motor electrical and inertia inputs retain their stated units.

Can I print the results without the explanatory article?

Yes. Print / Save PDF exports a dedicated report for the active calculator, or all valid sections if selected. Navigation, share controls and explanatory article content are excluded.

Can I use the results directly in Marlin, GRBL or Klipper?

The CNC tab shows reviewed geometry examples, not a complete machine configuration. Check axis assignment, reduction convention, current, limits and firmware documentation before applying values.
