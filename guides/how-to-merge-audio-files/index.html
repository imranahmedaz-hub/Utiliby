What does it mean to merge audio files?

Merging audio files means taking two or more separate recordings and combining them into one continuous file. People also call it joining, combining, concatenating or stitching audio. The result plays from start to finish as a single track, so you no longer have to switch between files.

There are two quite different things that people mean by "merge". The first is joining files one after another, for example gluing podcast segments or audiobook chapters into one episode. The second is layering files on top of each other, for example putting a voice recording over a music bed so that both play at the same time. This guide covers both, and explains which join method suits which job.

You do not need to install editing software for either task. A browser-based tool such as the free Utiliby Audio Merger can decode your files, join them with the settings you choose and give you a finished file to download.

How to merge audio files online, step by step

These steps use the Audio Merger, but the same order of decisions applies to any audio joiner.

Step 1: Add your audio files

Click the upload area or drag your files onto it. The tool accepts MP3, WAV, OGG, M4A, AAC and FLAC, and also Opus and WebM audio. You can mix formats in one project, add up to 50 files and use files of up to 300 MB each.

Step 2: Put the clips in order

Drag each clip by its grip handle or use the up and down buttons. If your files are named with numbers, the Sort menu can order them by name using natural numbering, so "track 2" comes before "track 10", or by length. Checking the order now saves you from re-exporting later.

Step 3: Trim and adjust each clip

Drag the round handles on the waveform to cut dead air from the start or end, or type exact times such as 1:23.45. Click the waveform to start playback from that point. You can also duplicate a clip, for example to repeat a jingle, and raise or lower the gain of a single clip by up to 30 dB if one recording is much quieter than the others.

Step 4: Choose how the clips join

Pick one of four join modes (explained in the next section): seamless, silence gap, crossfade or overlay. If you are unsure, the quick presets set sensible defaults for a podcast episode, a music mix or album, a DJ-style blend, audiobook chapters, voice memos and interviews, a gapless lossless join, or layered tracks. A live timeline previews the result before you export.

Step 5: Set fades, loudness and silence handling

Optional processing includes a global fade in and fade out, loudness matching, peak normalisation, removal of silence at clip edges (or long internal pauses), a very short fade at each join to prevent clicks, and a clipping guard. Each is covered in the sections below.

Step 6: Choose the output and merge

Choose WAV (16-bit, 24-bit or 32-bit float, lossless) or MP3 (64 to 320 kbps). You can also set the sample rate (Auto, 48, 44.1, 32, 22.05, 16 or 8 kHz), stereo or mono, and the file name. Then press Merge audio, listen to the result and download it.

Tip: Listen to the first and last few seconds of every join before you publish. Problems almost always show up at the joins.

Which join mode should you use?

The join mode decides what happens at the point where one clip ends and the next begins. Choosing the right one is the biggest difference between a merge that sounds professional and one that sounds like a rough cut.

Join modeWhat it doesBest for
SeamlessClips play back to back with no pauseContinuous recordings split into parts, gapless albums, lossless joins
Silence gapInserts a pause of a length you set between clipsPodcast segments, audiobook chapters, interviews, voice memos
CrossfadeOverlaps the end of one clip with the start of the next while one fades out and the other fades inMusic mixes, playlists, any join where a hard cut sounds abrupt
OverlayEvery clip starts at 0:00 and all clips are summed so they play togetherVoice over music, layering sound effects or loops

Equal-power versus linear crossfade

The crossfade curve can be equal-power or linear. An equal-power curve is designed to keep the perceived loudness steady through the overlap, which usually sounds smoother when joining two different songs. A linear curve is the simple straight-line fade and is more suitable when the two clips are very similar or are the same recording. The length of a crossfade is limited to half the length of the shorter neighbouring clip, so very short clips cannot be crossfaded by long amounts.

How long should a crossfade be?

There is no single correct value. As a starting point, try two to six seconds for music, and well under a second for speech, then listen and adjust. Longer crossfades blend more, but they also overlap more of each clip, so you can end up hearing the intro of the next track while the previous one is still finishing its final note.

WAV or MP3: which output format should you choose?

The output format is a trade-off between quality and file size. WAV stores the audio uncompressed, so it is lossless but large. MP3 is compressed, so it is much smaller but discards some audio information.

Why a merged MP3 can sound slightly worse

When you merge MP3 files, the tool first decodes each one to raw audio, joins them, and then encodes the combined audio again. That second compression pass adds a small additional loss on top of the loss already in your sources. For material you will keep editing, export WAV. For material you will publish and not touch again, export MP3 at a high bitrate; 256 or 320 kbps leaves little audible difference for most listeners and most content.

Why gapless joins need WAV

MP3 encoders add a very small amount of padding at the start and end of a file. When the files are played back to back, that padding can create a tiny gap. If you are joining tracks that must flow without any break, such as a live album or a continuous mix, use the seamless mode and export WAV.

Sample rate and channels

If you leave the sample rate on Auto, the tool uses your browser's rate, which is commonly 44.1 or 48 kHz. Pick 44.1 kHz for music that will be distributed as audio, 48 kHz for audio that will go into video, and a lower rate only for speech where the file size matters. Choose mono for single-voice recordings: it halves the file size without losing anything that was in a mono source.

Merging podcast segments

Record or export your intro, interview and outro as separate clips, trim the dead air from each, and join them with a short pause so the transitions feel natural. Matching loudness matters most here, because a guest recorded on a different microphone often sounds noticeably quieter than the host.

Joining audiobook chapters

Chapters usually arrive as many numbered files. The natural-number sort puts them in the right order, a silence gap of a second or two gives the listener a breath between chapters, and exporting as mono keeps the file size reasonable for long listening.

Building a music mix

For a continuous mix, use a crossfade and listen for the two tracks clashing in tempo or key during the overlap. If they clash, shorten the crossfade or trim the intro of the second track so the overlap lands on a quieter section.

How to stop the volume jumping between clips

The most common complaint about merged audio is that one clip is suddenly much louder or quieter than its neighbour. This happens because the sources were recorded or mastered at different levels. Three settings address it.

  • Per-clip gain: raise or lower a single clip by hand, by up to 30 dB. Use this when one clip is obviously off.
  • Match clip loudness: the tool measures the average level of each clip and moves them toward a common level, limited to plus or minus 20 dB.
  • Peak normalise: after the merge, the whole file is scaled so its loudest peak sits at −1 dBFS, just below the point where digital audio distorts.

Matching loudness and peak normalising can be used together: first make the clips agree with each other, then set the overall level of the finished file. Keep in mind that these are estimates of loudness, not certified broadcast LUFS metering, so if you must meet a specific loudness standard for a broadcaster or platform, check the final file with a dedicated meter.

How to get the best quality from a merge

  • Start from the best source files you have. A merge cannot restore detail that a low-bitrate source has already lost.
  • Do all your editing in one pass. Every extra decode and re-encode of an MP3 adds loss, so trim, order and merge in a single export instead of merging in several rounds.
  • Export WAV for anything you will edit again. Convert to MP3 only as the very last step.
  • Turn on dither for 16-bit and MP3 output. Dither is a tiny amount of noise added when reducing bit depth, and it makes quiet passages sound smoother.
  • Use the click guard. A very short fade, around 3 ms, at each join prevents the tiny pop that can occur when a waveform is cut mid-cycle.
  • Keep the clipping guard on. When clips are summed or boosted, the result can exceed the maximum level and distort; the guard prevents that.

Limits you should know about

In the Audio Merger, you can add up to 50 files of up to 300 MB each. Because the audio is decoded into uncompressed form in your device's memory while it is processed, long projects need a lot of RAM: roughly 600 MB for one hour of stereo audio. The tool warns you above about 1 GB of estimated memory, which is roughly an hour and a half of stereo audio, and refuses jobs above about 2.5 GB, which is roughly four hours. WAV files are also limited to about 4 GB by the format, which is roughly six hours of 16-bit, 44.1 kHz stereo. Phones have the least memory, so for long projects a laptop or desktop computer is the safer choice.

Is it safe to merge audio files online?

Many online audio joiners upload your files to a server, process them there and send the result back. That means your recordings leave your device, which matters if they contain interviews, voice memos, client calls or unreleased music.

The Utiliby Audio Merger works differently. Your files are decoded and mixed locally in your browser using the Web Audio API and JavaScript, so the audio stays on your device. The one network request the tool describes is a one-time download of the open-source MP3 encoder library (lamejs) from a public CDN, and only when you choose MP3 output. WAV export works offline once the page has loaded. The optional "remember settings" feature stores only your settings in the browser, never your audio, and a saved project file contains only settings, order, trims and gains.

As with any website, it is sensible to keep a backup of your original files and to check the finished file before you publish it.

A note on copyright

Merging audio is a technical operation, and it does not change who owns the recordings. Make sure you have the right to use and combine the audio you are working with, especially if you plan to publish or share the result. Your own recordings, audio you have licensed, and material released under a licence that allows modification are the safest to work with.

What an audio merger does not do

A merger joins and levels audio; it is not a full audio editor. The Audio Merger does not remove background noise, apply compression or equalisation, or change pitch or tempo. If your recordings have hum, hiss or room echo, clean them in a dedicated editor first and then merge the cleaned files.

Does merging MP3 files reduce quality?

It can. An MP3 is already compressed, and a merge decodes the files and encodes the result again, which adds one more round of lossy compression. To avoid extra loss, export as WAV (lossless), or export MP3 at a high bitrate such as 256 or 320 kbps.

How do I join audio files without a gap?

Use the seamless join mode and export as WAV. MP3 encoders add tiny amounts of padding at the start and end of a file, so a merged MP3 can contain a very short gap that a WAV export does not.

What is a crossfade and when should I use one?

A crossfade overlaps the end of one clip with the start of the next while the first fades out and the second fades in. It suits music mixes and any join where a hard cut would sound abrupt. A length of a few seconds is a common starting point for music, and shorter for speech.

Why does the volume jump between my clips?
