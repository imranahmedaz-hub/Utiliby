Can you refinance with bad credit, self-employed income or negative equity?

Often you can, but the options narrow and the cost rises. Lenders judge a refinance on three things: your credit history, your income and how much equity the home has. Weakness in one can sometimes be offset by strength in another, and some programs are built for borrowers who would not qualify for a standard loan. This guide explains what each problem means, which options are commonly available in the US, what to check first and how to test whether a refinance still pays.

Note: Eligibility rules, minimum scores and program details change often and differ by lender and loan. The figures below are typical, not guarantees. Ask lenders for current requirements and consider speaking to a housing counselor or licensed loan officer.

Refinancing with bad credit

A lower credit score usually means a higher interest rate, and below certain scores some loan types are unavailable. Many conventional lenders look for a score of at least the low 600s, while government-backed streamline programs can be more flexible because they focus on your payment history on the existing loan. A score that has recently improved, or a short spell of missed payments long ago, may matter less than a recent one.

Before you apply, get your credit reports, which you can request free from the main credit bureaus, and check them for errors. Dispute anything wrong, pay down card balances to lower your credit use, and avoid opening new credit before the application. Even a small rise in your score can move you into a lower rate tier, which matters more than it seems on a large loan. The Mortgage Refinance Calculator has a rate tab that shows how much each step in rate changes your savings and break-even.

How a higher rate changes the maths

The question with a weaker profile is not only whether you can refinance, but whether you should. A refinance that lowers your rate by half a point may still pay if you have a large balance, but if bad credit means the best offer is close to your current rate, the costs may never be recovered. The break-even tells you directly.

ExampleA homeowner owes 250,000 at 7.5% with 27 years left, and is offered 7.0% with 5,000 of costs. The payment falls by about 80 a month, so the break-even is about 61 months, a little over five years. If the homeowner expects to move in three years, the refinance loses money. If the offer improves to 6.5%, the payment falls by about 160 and the break-even is about 31 months.

Use the Refinance Break-Even Calculator to test your own offer, and ask whether improving your credit first, or waiting a few months, could change the result.

Refinancing when you are self-employed

Self-employed borrowers can refinance, but lenders want more proof of income. A common requirement is two years of personal and business tax returns, and lenders usually average your net income after business expenses. Because tax deductions lower your taxable income, you may qualify for less than your real cash income suggests. Lenders may also ask for a profit and loss statement, a business licence and bank statements.

Some lenders offer alternative documentation loans, such as those based on bank statements, which can suit borrowers with irregular or heavily written-off income. They tend to carry higher rates and larger down payment or equity requirements, so check the total cost carefully. If you are close to qualifying, a few simple steps can help: keep personal and business accounts separate, keep returns and statements up to date, and avoid large unexplained deposits.

Refinancing with negative equity (underwater)

You have negative equity when you owe more than the home is worth. Standard refinances usually require some equity, because the lender wants the loan to be less than the value. That makes an underwater refinance hard, but not always impossible.

The options depend on who holds or insures your loan. Loans owned by the large government-sponsored enterprises may be eligible for special high loan-to-value refinance options that allow a limited increase in loan-to-value, though they have conditions on payment history and other factors. Government-insured loans, such as FHA, VA and USDA, have streamline programs that may not need a new appraisal. The earlier federal program for underwater borrowers ended in 2018, so check which current programs apply. See the FHA, VA and USDA Streamline Refinance Calculator for those programs.

What to check before you apply

  1. Your credit reports and scores. Fix errors and reduce card balances.
  2. Your loan type and owner. Your servicer can tell you whether it is FHA, VA, USDA or conventional, and who owns it.
  3. Your equity. Estimate the home's value from recent nearby sales and compare it with your balance.
  4. Your income documents. Gather returns, bank statements and profit and loss statements.
  5. Your payment history. A clean last twelve months helps with many programs.
  6. Your goal. Is it a lower payment, a lower rate or cash? Each leads to different options.

A step-by-step plan

If your profile is weaker than you would like, a short plan can improve your chances and your offers. Start about three to six months before you want to apply if you can.

  1. Month 1: Take stock. Pull your credit reports, list your debts, confirm your loan type and estimate your home's value.
  2. Months 1 to 3: Strengthen the file. Fix errors, pay cards down below about a third of their limits if you can, and avoid new credit. Gather two years of returns and recent statements.
  3. Month 3: Get quotes. Ask at least three lenders for a Loan Estimate on the same day, including a lender that offers government-backed streamlines and, if relevant, one that does bank-statement loans.
  4. Month 3: Test the numbers. Put each quote into the Mortgage Refinance Calculator and compare the break-even with how long you will stay.
  5. Decide. If no offer clears the break-even, wait, keep rebuilding and try again, or pay extra principal to build equity.

Waiting is a legitimate result. A refinance that does not pay is worse than no refinance, and an improved score or extra equity can change the offers you receive.

Documents lenders commonly ask for

  • Government ID and Social Security number for the credit check.
  • Recent pay stubs and two years of W-2 forms for employees, or two years of personal and business returns for the self-employed.
  • Two to three months of bank and investment statements.
  • Your latest mortgage statement and proof of homeowners insurance.
  • A profit and loss statement and business licence for the self-employed.
  • An explanation letter for any late payments, gaps in employment or large deposits.

Having these ready speeds the process and avoids last-minute surprises that can delay a rate lock.

Alternatives to refinancing

  • Ask your servicer about a modification if you are struggling to pay. It can change the rate or term without a new loan.
  • Pay down the balance to build equity, which can unlock better options and end mortgage insurance. See the Remove PMI Refinance Calculator.
  • Wait and rebuild. A few months of on-time payments and lower card balances can raise your score.
  • Talk to a HUD-approved housing counselor. Counseling is often free or low cost.
  • Be careful with offers that promise approval for anyone. Check fees, and never pay large upfront charges for a promise of a refinance.

Warning signs and costs to avoid

Borrowers with weaker credit are more often targeted by costly offers. Be wary of lenders who ask for fees before they explain the loan, who pressure you to sign quickly, who leave blanks in documents, or who promise a rate before they check your credit. Read every figure on the Loan Estimate, compare the annual percentage rate, and ask for a plain-language explanation of any prepayment penalty or balloon payment. If a refinance would raise your payment or your total interest, make sure you understand why before you proceed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I refinance with a low credit score?

Often yes, but with a higher rate and fewer loan types. Some government-backed streamline programs focus more on your payment history than your score. Ask several lenders and compare the total cost.

What credit score do I need to refinance?

It varies by lender and loan. Many conventional lenders look for a score in the low 600s or higher, while some government-backed programs can be more flexible. A higher score usually gives a better rate.

Is it worth refinancing if my rate would barely fall?

Only if the savings repay the costs within the time you will keep the loan. A small rate drop with high costs can lose money. Use a break-even calculator to check.

Can I refinance if I am self-employed?

Yes. Lenders usually want two years of personal and business tax returns and use your net income after expenses. Some offer bank-statement loans for irregular or heavily written-off income, usually at a higher cost.

Why does my business income look lower to lenders?

Lenders use income after business deductions, because that is the income on your tax return. Large write-offs lower the figure they use.

Can I refinance if I owe more than my home is worth?

It is difficult, but options may exist. Some loans owned by large mortgage investors may qualify for high loan-to-value refinance options, and FHA, VA and USDA loans have streamline programs. Check which apply to your loan.

What happened to the HARP program?

The Home Affordable Refinance Program ended at the end of 2018. Some later options for high loan-to-value borrowers exist for eligible loans. Ask your servicer which you can use.

How can I raise my credit score before I refinance?

Check your reports for errors, pay your bills on time, reduce credit card balances and avoid new credit applications. Improvements can take a few months to show.

Should I use a co-signer?

A co-borrower with stronger credit or income can help you qualify, but they share responsibility for the loan. Be sure both parties understand the risk.

Where can I get free help?

HUD-approved housing counseling agencies offer free or low-cost advice on refinancing, modifications and avoiding scams. Your servicer can also explain hardship options.
